3.2  Laminar Flow and the Parabolic Velocity Profile

Where the fourth power actually comes from. Two factors of r squared, not one arbitrary exponent.

On finishing this section you should be able to
  • Describe laminar flow and state the no-slip condition.
  • Derive the parabolic velocity profile from the balance of pressure and shear.
  • Show that mean velocity is half peak velocity in laminar flow.
  • Derive the r⁴ dependence as the product of two independent r² terms.

What laminar means

Fluid moves in concentric cylindrical layers that slide over one another without mixing. Each layer has its own velocity, and adjacent layers exert viscous shear on each other. Nothing crosses between them.

At the wall, the fluid velocity is zero. This is the no-slip condition, and it is the boundary condition the whole derivation hangs on. Fluid in contact with a solid surface does not move relative to it.

The derivation

Consider a cylindrical shell of fluid at radius r inside a tube of radius R. Two forces act on it in steady flow, and they must balance.

Pressure difference across the ends of the cylinder pushes it forward: force = ΔP × πr².
Viscous shear at its outer surface drags it back: force = η × (dv/dr) × 2πrL.
Set them equal and rearrange: dv/dr = −ΔP·r / (2ηL).
Integrate with respect to r, applying v = 0 at r = R.
v(r) = (ΔP / 4ηL)(R² − r²).
v(r)  =  (ΔP / 4ηL)(R² − r²) A parabola. Zero at the wall where r = R, maximum at the axis where r = 0, giving vmax = ΔP·R² / 4ηL.
Mean velocity is exactly half peak velocity

Integrating the parabolic profile over the cross-section and dividing by area gives a mean of exactly vmax/2. This is not an approximation, it is a property of the parabola. It matters clinically because a Doppler probe on the axis of a vessel reads peak velocity, and converting that to volumetric flow requires halving it. Reporting peak velocity as if it were mean overestimates flow by a factor of two.

Where the fourth power comes from

Flow is mean velocity times cross-sectional area. Both terms carry an r².

Cross-sectional area A = πr². That is the first r².
Mean velocity = vmax/2 = ΔP·R² / 8ηL. That is the second r².
Q = A × v̄ = πr² × (ΔP·r² / 8ηL) = πΔP·r⁴ / 8ηL.
Discrimination trap

The second r² is the one people miss, and it is the interesting one. Widening the tube does not merely give the fluid more room, it makes the fluid move faster for the same pressure gradient, because the same wall shear is now distributed across more fluid. Area alone would give r². The velocity effect doubles the exponent.

So doubling the radius gives 4× the area and 4× the mean velocity, and 4 × 4 = 16.

Why is velocity zero at the wall rather than merely slow?

The no-slip condition. At the molecular level, fluid molecules adjacent to the surface are bound by intermolecular forces to the solid and share its velocity, which for a stationary tube is zero. This is an empirical boundary condition that holds for essentially all ordinary fluids, and every viscous flow derivation depends on it.

An ultrasound reads a peak velocity of 120 cm/s in a vessel with laminar flow. What is the mean velocity?

60 cm/s, exactly half, because the profile is parabolic. Using 120 to compute volumetric flow would overestimate it twofold. In turbulent flow the profile is much flatter and mean approaches peak, so the correction factor is regime-dependent.
