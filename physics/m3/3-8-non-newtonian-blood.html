3.8  Non-Newtonian Behaviour of Blood

Every assumption in this module fails in the circulation. Here is how, and why the model survives anyway.

On finishing this section you should be able to
  • Define Newtonian and non-Newtonian fluids in terms of shear rate.
  • Explain shear thinning in blood and its mechanism.
  • Describe the Fahraeus-Lindqvist effect and its reversal in capillaries.
  • Identify haematocrit as the dominant determinant of blood viscosity.

Newtonian and not

A Newtonian fluid has a viscosity independent of shear rate. Water is Newtonian. Plasma is very nearly Newtonian.

Whole blood is not. It is shear-thinning, meaning apparent viscosity falls as shear rate rises. At low flow it is thick; at high flow it thins.

At low shear rates, erythrocytes aggregate into stacked rouleaux and the plasma-cell suspension behaves as a thick, cohesive fluid.
Raising shear rate breaks the aggregates apart.
At higher shear still, individual erythrocytes deform into elongated shapes and align with the flow.
Both effects reduce internal friction, so apparent viscosity falls.
Clinical anchor

This behaviour is protective and it points the wrong way in shock. In low-flow states, shear rate falls, viscosity rises, and resistance increases exactly when perfusion is already inadequate. Rouleaux formation and sludging in the microcirculation are the visible form of this, and they are part of why low-output states become self-reinforcing.

The Fahraeus-Lindqvist effect

Apparent viscosity of blood decreases as vessel diameter falls below roughly 300 μm. This is counterintuitive and the mechanism is geometric rather than chemical.

Erythrocytes migrate away from the wall toward the axial stream, where shear is lowest.
This leaves a cell-poor plasma layer against the vessel wall.
Plasma is far less viscous than whole blood.
The high-shear region at the wall is therefore lubricated by low-viscosity fluid.
Apparent viscosity of the whole vessel falls.
Discrimination trap

The effect reverses below about 5 to 7 μm. Once vessel diameter approaches erythrocyte diameter, cells must deform substantially to pass single file, and apparent viscosity climbs steeply again. This is the inverse Fahraeus-Lindqvist effect, and it is why erythrocyte deformability, not just haematocrit, determines capillary perfusion. Loss of deformability in sepsis and in sickle cell disease impairs microcirculatory flow independently of any change in vessel calibre.

Haematocrit dominates

Of everything that determines blood viscosity, haematocrit is by far the largest term. The relationship is steeply non-linear at high values.

Deliberate haemodilution on cardiopulmonary bypass exploits this directly: reducing haematocrit lowers viscosity, which lowers resistance and improves microcirculatory flow during non-pulsatile hypothermic perfusion, at the cost of oxygen carrying capacity. It is an explicit trade between two terms.

Where this leaves Poiseuille

AssumptionViolated by
Newtonian fluidShear thinning, rouleaux, Fahraeus-Lindqvist
Rigid tubeVascular compliance; radius varies with transmural pressure
Steady flowPulsatility; impedance rather than resistance
Laminar flowTurbulence at valves, stenoses, and the great vessels

All four are false in vivo, and Poiseuille remains the right first-order model because the corrections are mostly modest and the r⁴ term is so dominant that it survives them. Knowing which assumption has failed tells you which direction the real answer lies from the calculated one.

Why does viscosity rise in shock, and why does that matter?

Low flow means low shear rate. Blood is shear-thinning, so falling shear raises apparent viscosity, and rising viscosity raises resistance by the η term in Poiseuille. Perfusion pressure must rise to maintain the same flow, at a point when the circulation cannot supply it. The effect is self-reinforcing and it is one mechanism by which low-output states deteriorate rather than plateau.

Why does the Fahraeus-Lindqvist effect reverse in capillaries?

Because there is no longer room for a cell-poor marginal layer. When vessel diameter approaches erythrocyte diameter, cells traverse single file and must deform to pass at all. The energy cost of deformation replaces the lubrication benefit, and apparent viscosity climbs steeply.
