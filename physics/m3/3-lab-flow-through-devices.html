Lab 3  ·  Flow Through Real Devices

Ratio calculations on equipment you will actually hold. Work each on paper before revealing.

Lab objectives
  • Compute relative flow and resistance from radius, length, and viscosity ratios.
  • Calculate a Reynolds number for gas flow in an endotracheal tube.
  • Quantify the heliox effect on flow regime.
  • Compute vascular resistance from haemodynamic data.
Reference data

Q ∝ r⁴ΔP/ηL · Re = ρvD/η · Air: ρ = 1.2 kg/m³, η = 1.8 × 10⁻⁵ Pa·s · Heliox 80:20: ρ ≈ 0.43 kg/m³, η ≈ 2.0 × 10⁻⁵ Pa·s · SVR = [(MAP − CVP)/CO] × 80

Problem 1

Compare flow through a 14 g cannula (internal radius ~1.0 mm) with an 18 g cannula (internal radius ~0.6 mm), same length and pressure.

Work it

Ratio = (1.0 / 0.6)⁴ = (1.667)⁴ = 7.7×.

A 40% increase in radius produces nearly eight times the flow. This is the single most useful piece of arithmetic in the module.

Problem 2

A 16 g peripheral cannula is 45 mm long with radius 0.8 mm. One lumen of a triple-lumen central catheter is 200 mm long with radius 0.5 mm. Same driving pressure. Compare.

Work it

Radius term: (0.8 / 0.5)⁴ = 1.6⁴ = 6.55 in favour of the peripheral.

Length term: 200 / 45 = 4.44, also in favour of the peripheral.

Combined: 6.55 × 4.44 = 29×.

The peripheral line delivers roughly thirty times the flow. The belief that central access is the resuscitation line is not slightly wrong, it is wrong by more than an order of magnitude.

Problem 3

A child's cricoid ring has an internal radius of 2.0 mm. Circumferential oedema reduces it by 1.0 mm. Compare with an adult trachea of radius 8.0 mm losing the same 1.0 mm.

Work it

Child: (2.0 / 1.0)⁴ = 16× resistance increase.

Adult: (8.0 / 7.0)⁴ = 1.70, so a 70% increase.

The same millimetre of oedema is an emergency in one airway and an inconvenience in the other. And because the child's flow will also become turbulent, the true penalty exceeds 16-fold, since turbulent flow carries an r⁵ dependence.

Problem 4

Gas flows through an 8.0 mm internal diameter endotracheal tube at 30 L/min. Compute the Reynolds number and state the regime.

Work it

Q = 30 L/min = 5.0 × 10⁻⁴ m³/s.

A = π(0.004)² = 5.03 × 10⁻⁵ m².

v = Q/A = 5.0 × 10⁻⁴ / 5.03 × 10⁻⁵ = 9.94 m/s.

Re = (1.2 × 9.94 × 0.008) / 1.8 × 10⁻⁵ = 0.0954 / 1.8 × 10⁻⁵ = 5300.

Above 4000, so turbulent. Poiseuille does not describe this flow, which is why measured work of breathing through a tube exceeds laminar predictions.

Problem 5

Same tube, same flow, now delivering heliox 80:20. What happens to the regime?

Work it

Velocity and diameter are unchanged. Only ρ and η change.

Re = (0.43 × 9.94 × 0.008) / 2.0 × 10⁻⁵ = 0.0342 / 2.0 × 10⁻⁵ = 1710.

Below 2000, so flow has returned to laminar. The Q² penalty disappears entirely. This is the mechanism of heliox stated numerically, and note that it required no change to the airway itself.

Problem 6

MAP 65, CVP 12, cardiac output 3.2 L/min. Compute SVR and comment.

Work it

SVR = [(65 − 12) / 3.2] × 80 = (53 / 3.2) × 80 = 16.56 × 80 = 1325 dyn·s·cm⁻⁵.

High-normal resistance with a low cardiac output and a low MAP. This is a pump problem with appropriate compensatory vasoconstriction, not vasodilatory shock. Giving a vasoconstrictor here treats the wrong term.

Problem 7

You cannot change cannula size. Name every remaining lever from Poiseuille and quantify the best case.

Work it

Raise ΔP: a pressure bag at 300 mmHg against a gravity head of roughly 40 mmHg is a 7-fold increase in gradient, so up to 7× flow.

Cut L: removing a 1 m extension set from a 1.5 m circuit gives 3× flow.

Lower η: warming fluid from 20 °C to 37 °C lowers viscosity by roughly 30%, so about 1.4×.

All three are multiplicative and none requires a new cannula. The pressure bag alone typically dominates.
