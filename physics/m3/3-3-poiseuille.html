3.3  Poiseuille's Law in Full

Radius gets all the attention. Length and viscosity are in the equation too, and they are the ones you can actually change.

On finishing this section you should be able to
  • State Poiseuille's law and identify the dependence of each term.
  • List the four assumptions the law requires.
  • Apply it to intravenous access and endotracheal tube selection.
  • Explain why a peripheral cannula outperforms a central line for volume resuscitation.
Q  =  πΔP r⁴ / 8ηL Q = flow · ΔP = pressure gradient · r = radius · η = dynamic viscosity · L = length. Resistance is the reciprocal: R = 8ηL / πr⁴.

Discrimination trap

Radius dominates, which is why it is the only term most people recall. But radius is usually fixed by anatomy or by the largest cannula that will go in, whereas length and pressure gradient are frequently under your direct control. Shortening tubing and pressurising a bag are interventions. Wishing for a larger vein is not.

The intravenous access case

This is the canonical application and the intuition runs backwards for most people.

A 16-gauge peripheral cannula is short, perhaps 45 mm, with a lumen matching its stated gauge.
A 16-gauge triple-lumen central catheter is long, perhaps 200 mm, and each individual lumen is far narrower than the catheter's outer gauge implies.
Length alone imposes a fourfold penalty.
The narrower lumen imposes a fourth-power penalty on top of that.
The peripheral line wins decisively, often by an order of magnitude.
Clinical anchor

For rapid volume resuscitation you want short and fat, not central. Two large peripheral cannulae beat a triple-lumen central line, and a dedicated large-bore introducer sheath beats both. The same reasoning condemns long micro-bore extension sets and unnecessary in-line filters, each of which quietly adds length and reduces radius on a line you are counting on.

Central access is for drug delivery, monitoring, and vesicant infusion. It is not a resuscitation line, and the belief that it is has cost patients.

The endotracheal tube case

Resistance scales with the inverse fourth power of radius, so tube size choices compound sharply.

R6.0 / R8.0  =  (4.0 / 3.0)4  =  3.16 A 6.0 mm tube presents more than three times the resistance of an 8.0 mm tube. In a spontaneously breathing patient, that resistance is work the patient performs.

The four assumptions

  1. Newtonian fluid. Viscosity independent of shear rate. Blood is not (3.8).
  2. Rigid tube. Radius fixed. Vessels are compliant.
  3. Laminar flow. Fails above the Reynolds transition (3.4).
  4. Steady, fully developed flow. Not pulsatile, and past the entrance length where the parabolic profile has established.

All four are violated somewhere in the circulation, which is why Poiseuille is a good model of the vasculature rather than a description of it.

You need to give 2 litres fast and have both a 20 g peripheral and a triple-lumen central. Which, and what else do you do?

Neither is adequate. Place a large-bore peripheral, 16 g or 14 g, or an introducer sheath. Meanwhile use what you have with the levers Poiseuille gives you: pressurise the bag to raise ΔP, remove any extension tubing to cut L, and use the largest available lumen of the central line. Warming the fluid also helps slightly by lowering viscosity.

Why does halving tubing length help less than going up one cannula size?

Length is inverse linear, so halving it doubles flow. Going from 20 g to 18 g raises radius by roughly 25%, and 1.25⁴ ≈ 2.4. The cannula change wins, and the two are multiplicative, so do both.

Q1
For rapid volume resuscitation, the most effective access is:
MechanismFlow rises with the fourth power of radius and falls with length. Central catheters are long with narrow individual lumens, which penalises them on both terms.
Q2
Resistance of a 6.0 mm endotracheal tube compared with an 8.0 mm tube is approximately:
MechanismResistance scales with 1/r⁴, so (4.0/3.0)⁴ = 3.16. Radius, not diameter, is the term in the equation.
Q3
Which term in Poiseuille's law is most often available as a bedside intervention?
MechanismPressurising an infusion bag raises ΔP immediately. Radius is limited by the vessel and the largest cannula that will pass, and viscosity is a property of the fluid.
Q4
Poiseuille's law assumes the fluid is Newtonian and the tube is rigid.
MechanismBoth, along with laminar and fully developed steady flow. Blood violates the first and vessels violate the second, which is why the law models the circulation rather than describing it.
Q5
Doubling the length of infusion tubing changes flow by a factor of:
MechanismLength appears in the denominator to the first power, so doubling it halves flow. The fourth-power relationship belongs to radius alone.
Q6
Adding a long micro-bore extension set to a resuscitation line is harmful because it:
MechanismIt penalises two terms at once, one linearly and one to the fourth power, on the line you are relying on to deliver volume.
Q7
Write Poiseuille's law solved for flow.
MechanismQ = πΔPr⁴/8ηL. The r⁴ comes from area and mean velocity each contributing r², and the 8ηL carries the viscous and geometric penalty.
