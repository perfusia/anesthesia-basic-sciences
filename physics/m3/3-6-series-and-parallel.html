3.6  Resistance in Series and Parallel

Why one dilated arteriole does not produce sixteen times the cardiac output, and why the small airways are the quiet ones.

On finishing this section you should be able to
  • Apply series and parallel combination rules to fluid resistances.
  • Explain why a single segment's resistance change has limited systemic effect.
  • Explain why airway resistance is lowest in the small airways despite their tiny individual radius.
  • Relate parallel architecture to the silent progression of small airway disease.

The rules carry over unchanged

Series:   Rtotal = R1 + R2 + R3 … Same flow through each element, pressure drops add.
Parallel:   1/Rtotal = 1/R1 + 1/R2 + 1/R3 … Same pressure across each element, flows add. Total resistance is always less than the smallest individual resistance.
What you already have

Both rules and the intuition for why parallel paths reduce total resistance. The clinical content is entirely in which vascular and airway structures are in series and which are in parallel, and that arrangement is not always what the anatomy looks like.

Why the r⁴ intuition misleads at system level

Discrimination trap

Doubling a single vessel's radius gives 16× the flow through that vessel at a fixed pressure gradient across it. It does not give 16× systemic flow.

Once that vessel stops being the limiting resistance, the pressure gradient across it collapses, and the constraint moves elsewhere in the circuit. Poiseuille describes a segment. Systemic flow is a circuit problem, and the two are not the same question.

The circulation is massively parallel

Systemic organ beds sit in parallel with one another: cerebral, coronary, renal, splanchnic, muscular, cutaneous. That is why total SVR is far lower than any single bed's resistance, and why occluding one bed raises SVR only modestly.

Within a bed, the arrangement is series: artery to arteriole to capillary to venule to vein. Arterioles are the dominant term in that series, which is why they are called the resistance vessels and why small changes in arteriolar tone move blood pressure so effectively.

The airway result that surprises people

An individual terminal bronchiole has a tiny radius, so by r⁴ it should have enormous resistance. In fact the small airways contribute only around 10 to 20% of total airway resistance, and most resistance sits in the large and medium airways.

Each airway generation branches, roughly doubling the number of tubes.
Individual radius falls with each generation, but the number of parallel paths rises far faster.
Total cross-sectional area therefore increases enormously moving distally, by orders of magnitude.
Resistances in parallel combine reciprocally, so thousands of tiny tubes in parallel present very little total resistance.
Velocity falls sharply as area rises, which is also why distal flow is laminar and heliox does not help there.
Clinical anchor

Because small airways contribute so little to total resistance, disease can destroy a large fraction of them before spirometry changes measurably. This is the silent zone of the lung, and it is why early small airway disease in smokers is clinically occult and why FEV1 is an insensitive early marker.

The same parallel architecture explains the opposite case: obstruction of a single large airway, which has no parallel path around it, is immediately catastrophic.

Why does occluding the renal circulation raise SVR only modestly?

The organ beds are in parallel. Removing one path from a parallel network raises total resistance, but the remaining paths continue to carry flow, so the change is proportional to the fraction of total conductance that bed represented. Removing one element from a series circuit, by contrast, would stop flow entirely.

Arterioles have a larger radius than capillaries yet contribute far more resistance. Why?

Numbers and arrangement. There are vastly more capillaries in parallel than arterioles, so capillary resistance combines reciprocally down to a small total despite each individual vessel being tiny. Arterioles are fewer, so their parallel combination stays high. It is another instance of parallel architecture beating the r⁴ intuition.

Q1
Total resistance of several vessels in parallel is:
MechanismReciprocals add, so every additional parallel path increases total conductance. The result is always below the smallest single element.
Q2
Doubling the radius of one arteriole does not produce sixteen times systemic flow because:
MechanismOnce that segment is no longer limiting, the pressure drop across it falls and the constraint moves elsewhere in the circuit. Poiseuille describes a segment, not a network.
Q3
Small airways contribute only 10 to 20% of total airway resistance because they are:
MechanismBranching multiplies the number of parallel paths faster than radius falls, so total cross-sectional area rises enormously and reciprocal combination drives total resistance down.
Q4
Early small airway disease may be clinically silent because those airways contribute little to total resistance.
MechanismThis is the silent zone. Extensive small airway destruction can occur before total resistance and therefore spirometry change measurably.
Q5
Within a single organ bed, the dominant resistance element is the:
MechanismArterioles are fewer in number and highly muscular, so their parallel combination remains high. Capillaries are individually tiny but so numerous that their combined resistance is low.
Q6
Give the rule for combining two resistances in parallel.
MechanismReciprocals add because the same pressure drives each path and the flows sum. This is why total resistance always falls below the smallest element.
