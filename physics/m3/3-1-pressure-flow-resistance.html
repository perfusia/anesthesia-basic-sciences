3.1  Pressure, Flow, and Resistance

The hydraulic analogy, what it buys you, and the four places it quietly stops being true.

On finishing this section you should be able to
  • State the relationship between pressure gradient, flow, and resistance.
  • Explain why resistance is a derived quantity rather than a measured one.
  • Compute systemic and pulmonary vascular resistance from haemodynamic data.
  • Name the assumptions the hydraulic analogy makes and where each fails in vivo.

The analogy

Flow through a tube is driven by a pressure difference and opposed by resistance. Written out, it is Ohm's law with different letters.

ΔP  =  Q × R ΔP = pressure gradient across the segment · Q = flow, volume per unit time · R = resistance. Compare V = IR: voltage is a potential difference, current is charge per time, resistance opposes.
What you already have

All of it, from circuit analysis. The mapping is exact for the linear case: potential difference to pressure gradient, current to volumetric flow, resistance to resistance. Series and parallel combination rules carry over unchanged, which is section 3.6.

What does not carry over is the assumption that R is a constant of the component. In a circuit, a resistor's resistance is a property of the resistor. In a tube, resistance depends on the flow through it, which makes the equation implicit rather than explicit.

Resistance is calculated, never measured

There is no instrument that reads vascular resistance. It is inferred by rearranging the equation around two things you can measure, a pressure gradient and a flow.

SVR  =  [(MAP − CVP) / CO] × 80 Normal 800–1200 dyn·s·cm−5. The 80 converts mmHg·min·L−1 into dyn·s·cm−5.
PVR  =  [(MPAP − PAWP) / CO] × 80 Normal 40–180 dyn·s·cm−5, roughly a tenth of systemic, which is the whole design of the pulmonary circulation.
Clinical anchor

Because SVR is derived from cardiac output, an error in the CO measurement propagates directly into the resistance number. A thermodilution CO that reads falsely low produces a falsely high SVR, and the reflex is to treat a vasoconstriction that is not there. When SVR and the clinical picture disagree, distrust the derived number before the patient, and check the inputs.

Where the analogy fails

AssumptionRealityConsequence
Resistance is constantDepends on flow regime, laminar or turbulentΔP stops being linear in Q above the transition (3.4)
Tube is rigidVessels are compliantRadius is a function of transmural pressure, so R changes with P
Fluid is NewtonianBlood is shear-thinningViscosity falls as flow rises (3.8)
Flow is steadyArterial flow is pulsatileInertia and wave reflection contribute; impedance, not resistance

Every one of these makes the true relationship harder than ΔP = QR, and none of them makes the analogy useless. It is a first-order model. The rest of this module is a systematic tour of the corrections.

Why is pulmonary vascular resistance roughly a tenth of systemic when both circulations carry the same cardiac output?

Because the driving gradient is roughly a tenth. Mean pulmonary artery pressure is around 15 mmHg against a wedge of 8, so a gradient near 7, while the systemic side runs a mean of 93 against a CVP of 5, a gradient near 88. Same Q, one twelfth the ΔP, so one twelfth the R. The pulmonary bed achieves it with short, thin-walled, highly compliant, massively parallel vessels.

A patient's SVR is calculated at 1800 with a cardiac output of 2.1 L/min. What should you check before treating the number?

The cardiac output. SVR is inversely proportional to CO, so a low CO drives a high SVR arithmetically whether or not any vasoconstriction exists. Confirm the CO by an independent method before giving a vasodilator on the strength of a derived value.
