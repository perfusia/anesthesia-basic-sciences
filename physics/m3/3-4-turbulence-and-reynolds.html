3.4  Turbulent Flow and Reynolds Number

Everything in the last two sections stops applying above a threshold, and radius starts mattering more, not less.

On finishing this section you should be able to
  • State the Reynolds number and identify what each term represents.
  • Give the approximate thresholds for laminar, transitional, and turbulent flow.
  • Explain why pressure drop scales with the square of flow in turbulence.
  • Explain why radius carries a fifth-power dependence in turbulent conditions.

The dimensionless number

Reynolds number is the ratio of inertial forces to viscous forces in a flowing fluid. It has no units, which is what makes it a universal predictor of regime rather than a property of a particular system.

Re  =  ρ v D / η ρ = density · v = mean velocity · D = diameter · η = dynamic viscosity. Numerator is inertia, denominator is viscosity. Note that density appears, not viscosity alone.
Reynolds numberRegime
Below ~2000Laminar. Poiseuille applies.
~2000 to ~4000Transitional. Unstable, geometry-dependent.
Above ~4000Turbulent. Poiseuille does not apply.
What you already have

Dimensionless groups and the idea that a ratio of competing effects predicts qualitative behaviour. The only new content is the specific thresholds and the clinical geometries where the transition sits.

What changes above the transition

Flow stops moving in orderly layers. Eddies form and fluid mixes radially.
Energy is dissipated in the eddies rather than only in viscous shear at the wall.
Pressure drop stops being linear in flow and becomes roughly proportional to Q².
Viscosity stops being the dominant fluid property. Density takes over.
The velocity profile flattens, so mean velocity approaches peak rather than being half of it.
Radius goes to the fifth power

Run turbulent flow through the Darcy-Weisbach relation and the radius dependence comes out as r5, not r⁴. Turbulence makes radius matter more, which is the opposite of the usual intuition that turbulence somehow blurs the geometry.

Practically: a narrowing severe enough to make flow turbulent is punished twice, once for being narrow and again for the regime change it caused.

Clinical anchor

Gas moving through an endotracheal tube at ordinary minute ventilation is frequently transitional or frankly turbulent, particularly at the connector and at any bend. Compute it for a 8.0 mm tube at 30 L/min and Reynolds comes out near 5300. This is why measured work of breathing through a tube exceeds what a laminar calculation predicts, and why tube resistance is not simply a Poiseuille problem.

Stridor is the audible signature of turbulence. Sound requires the pressure fluctuations that eddies produce, so laminar flow is silent. A quiet patient with severe obstruction may be moving no air at all.

Discrimination trap

Turbulence is provoked by high velocity, high density, large diameter, and low viscosity. Three of those raise Re in the numerator and one lowers the denominator. The intuition that a narrow tube causes turbulence is backwards as stated: diameter is in the numerator, so a wide tube favours turbulence at a given velocity. What a stenosis does is force velocity up sharply, and the velocity term wins.

Why is turbulence audible and laminar flow silent?

Sound is a pressure fluctuation. Laminar flow is steady with no radial mixing, so it generates no fluctuation and no sound. Turbulent eddies produce rapid local pressure variation across a broad frequency range, which propagates as noise. Stridor, bruits, and Korotkoff sounds are all turbulence made audible.

A tracheal stenosis narrows the airway. Diameter is in the numerator of Re, so why does turbulence increase?

Because velocity is also in the numerator and rises much faster. For a fixed volumetric flow, velocity scales with 1/r², so halving the radius quadruples velocity while only halving the diameter. Net effect on Re is a doubling. The velocity term dominates.
