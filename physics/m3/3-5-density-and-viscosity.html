3.5  Density Against Viscosity, and Heliox

Which fluid property governs depends entirely on the regime, and that is the whole basis of a therapy that only works in one place.

On finishing this section you should be able to
  • State which fluid property governs resistance in laminar and in turbulent flow.
  • Explain the mechanism by which heliox reduces airway resistance.
  • Explain why heliox helps in large-airway obstruction and not in asthma.
  • State the oxygenation constraint that limits heliox use.

This is not a subtlety. It means an intervention that changes density will do essentially nothing in laminar conditions and a great deal in turbulent ones, and that single fact determines where heliox works.

Helium

PropertyNitrogenHeliumRatio
Density1.25 g/L0.18 g/L~1/7
Viscosity17.8 μPa·s19.9 μPa·s~1.1

Helium is roughly seven times less dense than nitrogen and very slightly more viscous. That combination is the entire pharmacology of the gas.

Substituting helium for nitrogen cuts density by a large factor.
Reynolds number falls proportionally, since ρ is in the numerator.
Flow that was turbulent may return to laminar, eliminating the Q² penalty entirely.
Where flow stays turbulent, resistance still falls because density governs it.
Where flow was already laminar, nothing improves. Viscosity governs there, and helium's viscosity is marginally worse.
Clinical anchor

Heliox helps: upper and large airway obstruction. Stridor, tracheal stenosis, post-extubation oedema, tumour, foreign body, croup. These are high-velocity turbulent lesions in large conduits, exactly where density governs.

Heliox does little: asthma and small airway disease. Flow in the distal airways is already laminar because velocity is low and total cross-sectional area is enormous. Viscosity governs there, and helium is not less viscous.

The distinction is a standard exam question, and the reasoning is entirely contained in the two equations from 3.3 and 3.4.

The constraint everyone forgets

Heliox is a mixture, and the benefit comes from the helium fraction. Commercial blends are 80:20 or 70:30 helium to oxygen, which caps FIO2 at 0.20 to 0.30. Below roughly 60% helium the density advantage largely disappears.

So heliox is unusable in a patient who needs a high FIO2. It is a therapy for obstruction, not for hypoxaemia, and the two frequently coexist. If the patient needs oxygen more than they need reduced resistance, heliox is the wrong choice.

A patient has severe post-extubation stridor and an SpO₂ of 88% on 60% oxygen. Is heliox indicated?

The lesion is right for heliox but the oxygen requirement forbids it. An 80:20 mixture would drop FIO2 to 0.20 and the patient would desaturate further. Secure the airway or treat the oedema. Heliox is for obstruction with adequate oxygenation, and this patient has neither.

Why does helium's slightly higher viscosity not undermine its use?

Because it is only deployed where turbulence dominates, and in turbulent flow viscosity is not the governing property. The tiny viscosity penalty is irrelevant against a sevenfold density advantage. In laminar flow the calculus reverses, which is precisely why it fails in small airway disease.
