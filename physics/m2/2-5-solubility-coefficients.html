2.5  Bunsen and Ostwald Solubility Coefficients

Two ways of quoting the same physical fact, and the reason anesthesia uses one of them and not the other.

On finishing this section you should be able to
  • Define the Bunsen and Ostwald solubility coefficients.
  • Explain why the Ostwald coefficient is independent of pressure.
  • State the relationship between the Ostwald coefficient and the partition coefficient.
  • Select the appropriate coefficient for a stated measurement condition.

Two definitions

Both answer the question how much gas dissolves in this liquid. They differ only in the conditions to which the dissolved volume is corrected.

Why the Ostwald coefficient is pressure-independent

This is the part worth deriving rather than accepting, and it falls straight out of Henry's law combined with Boyle's law.

Double the pressure. By Henry's law, twice as many gas molecules dissolve.
But by Boyle's law, the gas phase you are comparing against has also been compressed to half its volume.
The dissolved volume, measured at that same new pressure, is likewise halved.
The ratio of dissolved volume to liquid volume is therefore unchanged.
The Ostwald coefficient is a constant at a given temperature, whatever the pressure.

The Bunsen coefficient loses this property precisely because it corrects the dissolved volume back to STP, breaking the cancellation.

The identity that matters

At equilibrium the partial pressure of a gas is equal in both phases, by definition of equilibrium. Under that condition the Ostwald coefficient is the partition coefficient: the ratio of the concentration of the substance in one phase to its concentration in the other.

λblood:gas  =  [agent]blood / [agent]gas   at equal partial pressure

So every partition coefficient quoted in section 2.6 is an Ostwald coefficient wearing a clinical name. There is no second concept to learn.

Discrimination trap

The partition coefficient compares concentrations at equal partial pressures, not at equal volumes and not at equal total amounts. Equilibrium means the partial pressures have equalised, and it is precisely because the concentrations have not equalised that the coefficient is a useful number.

Why does temperature have to be stated with any solubility coefficient, while pressure does not for the Ostwald?

Because the underlying physical solubility genuinely changes with temperature, by the mechanism in section 2.4, and nothing cancels it. The pressure dependence cancels between Henry's law and Boyle's law, but there is no equivalent cancellation for temperature. Blood:gas coefficients are conventionally quoted at 37 °C for exactly this reason.

A blood:gas partition coefficient of 2.4 means what, precisely?

At equilibrium, when the partial pressure of the agent is identical in blood and in the gas phase, the concentration in blood is 2.4 times the concentration in the gas. Blood holds 2.4 times as much agent per unit volume at the same driving pressure, which is what makes the agent slow.

MechanismDoubling pressure doubles dissolved molecules by Henry's law and halves the compared gas volume by Boyle's law. The two effects cancel and the ratio is preserved.

MechanismBunsen corrects the dissolved volume back to STP, which breaks the pressure cancellation that makes the Ostwald coefficient constant.

MechanismEquilibrium is defined by equal partial pressures. The coefficient is useful precisely because the concentrations differ under that condition.
Q4
The blood:gas partition coefficient is an Ostwald coefficient applied to blood and gas phases.
MechanismThey are the same quantity. The partition coefficient is the Ostwald coefficient measured between the two phases of clinical interest, conventionally at 37 °C.
Q5
Solubility coefficients must always be quoted with a stated temperature because:
MechanismWarming shifts dissolved molecules back into the gas phase, and nothing cancels this effect the way pressure dependence cancels. Hence the 37 °C convention.
Q6
Which solubility coefficient corrects the dissolved gas volume to STP?
MechanismBunsen corrects to standard temperature and pressure. Ostwald measures at the actual conditions, which is what makes it pressure-independent and clinically useful.
