2.4  Henry's Law

How much gas dissolves, why warming a solution releases it, and why this is not the same thing as haemoglobin binding.

On finishing this section you should be able to
  • State Henry's law and apply it to dissolved oxygen content.
  • Explain why gas solubility falls as temperature rises.
  • Distinguish dissolved gas from chemically bound gas.
  • Apply Henry's law to rewarming, decompression, and gas embolism.

The law

At constant temperature, the quantity of a gas dissolved in a liquid is directly proportional to the partial pressure of that gas in equilibrium with the liquid.

C  =  k × P C is dissolved concentration, P is partial pressure above the liquid, and k is a constant specific to that gas, that liquid, and that temperature.
Clinical anchor

Dissolved oxygen in plasma obeys Henry's law exactly, at 0.003 mL per dL per mmHg. At a PaO2 of 100 mmHg that is 0.3 mL/dL, against roughly 20 mL/dL carried on haemoglobin. Dissolved oxygen is a rounding error under normal conditions and becomes the entire oxygen supply under hyperbaric conditions, where a PaO2 of 2000 mmHg dissolves 6 mL/dL, enough to sustain tissue with no functioning haemoglobin at all. That is the physical basis of hyperbaric therapy for carbon monoxide poisoning.

The temperature relationship, which runs backwards from intuition

Gas solubility decreases as temperature rises. This is the opposite of the behaviour of most solids, where warming dissolves more.

A dissolved gas molecule is held in solution by weak interaction with the solvent.
Raising temperature increases the kinetic energy of every molecule in the liquid.
More dissolved molecules acquire enough energy to escape into the gas phase.
The equilibrium shifts out of solution, and solubility falls.
Discrimination trap

This is a genuine hazard on cardiopulmonary bypass. Blood cooled during bypass holds more dissolved gas. Rewarming it lowers solubility, and gas comes out of solution as bubbles precisely when the blood is being returned to the patient. It is the same physics as opening a warm carbonated drink, and it is why rewarming is deliberately gradual and why arterial line filters exist.

Why nitrous expands a pneumothorax

Both nitrous and nitrogen move down their partial pressure gradients, but Henry's law fixes how fast. Nitrous is roughly 34 times more soluble in blood than nitrogen, so blood delivers nitrous into an air-filled space vastly faster than it can carry nitrogen out. Volume rises in a compliant space such as bowel or a pneumothorax, and pressure rises in a non-compliant one such as the middle ear or a pneumocephalus.

Why does Henry's law not describe oxygen carriage on haemoglobin?

Because haemoglobin binding is a chemical reaction with a saturable binding site, not physical dissolution. It produces a sigmoid curve that plateaus, whereas Henry's law is strictly linear with no ceiling. Only the dissolved fraction, the 0.003 mL/dL/mmHg term, is Henry's law behaviour.

A blood gas sample sits at room temperature for an hour before analysis. Predict the error.

Leucocytes and erythrocytes keep metabolising, consuming oxygen and producing CO2, so measured PaO2 falls and PaCO2 rises. Icing the sample slows metabolism, and it also raises gas solubility, which is why iced samples must be analysed on a corrected basis rather than treated as identical to fresh ones.
