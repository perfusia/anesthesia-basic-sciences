Lab 2  ·  Agent Comparison and Prediction

Build the table from the coefficients, then predict behaviour from it. Do these on paper before revealing.

Lab objectives
  • Compute saturated vapour concentration for each agent.
  • Rank agents by onset speed and by potency from their coefficients alone.
  • Predict emergence behaviour for short and prolonged cases.
  • Apply the oil:gas × MAC relationship to estimate an unknown.
Reference data at 20 °C, 1 atm

Desflurane SVP 669, BP 22.8 °C, blood:gas 0.42, oil:gas 19, MAC 6.0 · Sevoflurane SVP 157, BP 58.6 °C, blood:gas 0.65, oil:gas 47, MAC 2.0 · Isoflurane SVP 238, BP 48.5 °C, blood:gas 1.4, oil:gas 91, MAC 1.15 · Halothane SVP 243, BP 50.2 °C, blood:gas 2.4, oil:gas 224, MAC 0.75 · Nitrous oxide blood:gas 0.47, oil:gas 1.4, MAC 104

Problem 1

Compute the saturated vapour concentration of each volatile agent at 20 °C and one atmosphere.

Work it

Desflurane 669/760 = 88% · Halothane 243/760 = 32% · Isoflurane 238/760 = 31% · Sevoflurane 157/760 = 21%

Note how far above clinical concentrations all of these sit. A vaporiser's job is dilution, not concentration, and desflurane's 88% is why splitting-ratio dilution becomes impractical for it.

Problem 2

Rank the five agents by speed of onset, fastest first, and justify from a single number.

Work it

Desflurane 0.42 → nitrous 0.47 → sevoflurane 0.65 → isoflurane 1.4 → halothane 2.4.

The number is blood:gas, and lower is faster. Low solubility means blood saturates quickly, so alveolar partial pressure rises rapidly toward inspired and the brain follows.

Problem 3

Rank the same five by potency, most potent first. Is the ordering the same?

Work it

Halothane 224 → isoflurane 91 → sevoflurane 47 → desflurane 19 → nitrous 1.4.

The number is oil:gas, and higher is more potent. The ordering is almost exactly reversed from onset speed, which is a coincidence of chemistry rather than a physical law: the same molecular properties that reduce lipid affinity also reduce blood solubility. Do not state it as a rule.

Problem 4

A novel agent has an oil:gas coefficient of 30. Estimate its MAC.

Work it

Using oil:gas × MAC ≈ 150: MAC ≈ 150 / 30 = 5%.

Sanity check against the real agents: their products range from 94 to 168, so an estimate of 3% to 5.6% is the honest interval. The Meyer-Overton correlation is strong enough for an order-of-magnitude prediction and not strong enough for a dose.

Problem 5

Two patients, one 25-minute case and one seven-hour case, both on sevoflurane. Predict how emergence differs and name the physical reason.

Work it

Short case: emergence is fast and governed almost entirely by the blood:gas coefficient of 0.65. Fat has barely begun to load.

Long case: emergence is markedly slower. Fat:blood for sevoflurane is roughly 27 and adipose is poorly perfused, so seven hours is long enough to load a large, slowly emptying reservoir that continues releasing agent after the vaporiser is off.

This is the mechanism behind desflurane's niche in prolonged bariatric surgery.

Problem 6

Why does an agent's blood:gas coefficient not predict its potency, given that both describe solubility?

Work it

Because they describe solubility in different phases, serving different functions. Blood:gas describes how much agent the blood reservoir must absorb before partial pressure rises, which is a kinetic question about speed. Oil:gas describes affinity for the lipid target, which is a pharmacodynamic question about effect at the site. Same physical quantity, different compartment, different clinical meaning.
