2.7  Diffusion, Fick's Law, and Graham's Law

Two laws that look similar, apply to different geometries, and give opposite answers about whether molecular weight matters.

On finishing this section you should be able to
  • State Fick's law of diffusion and identify each term.
  • State Graham's law and specify the geometry in which it applies.
  • Explain why carbon dioxide diffuses far faster than oxygen despite being heavier.
  • Predict which clinical scenarios are diffusion-limited.

Fick's law: diffusion through a membrane

Rate  ∝  (A × ΔP × s) / (d × √MW) A = surface area · ΔP = partial pressure gradient · s = solubility in the membrane · d = membrane thickness · MW = molecular weight

Read the structure rather than memorising the string. Rate rises with area, driving gradient, and solubility. It falls with distance and with the square root of molecular weight. Only two terms are in the denominator, and molecular weight sits under a square root, which caps how much it can ever matter.

Graham's law: effusion through an orifice

Rate  ∝  1 / √MW No solubility term, because there is no membrane to dissolve into.
Discrimination trap

The distinction is geometry, and it decides whether solubility appears at all.

  • Through a membrane: Fick. Solubility matters, and usually dominates.
  • Through an orifice: Graham. Only molecular weight matters.

The case that proves solubility dominates

Carbon dioxide has a molecular weight of 44 against oxygen's 32, so on molecular weight alone CO2 should diffuse more slowly, by a factor of √(32/44) = 0.85. In fact CO2 crosses the alveolar membrane roughly 20 times faster.

CO2 is about 24 times more soluble in plasma and membrane lipid than O2.
Fick's law puts solubility in the numerator, unmodified.
Molecular weight sits in the denominator under a square root, so its 15% penalty is small.
24 × 0.85 ≈ 20. Solubility wins decisively.
Clinical anchor

This is why a thickened alveolar membrane produces hypoxaemia long before it produces hypercapnia. Diffusion impairment from pulmonary fibrosis, oedema, or pneumonitis limits oxygen first, and CO2 continues crossing readily. A patient with a diffusion defect who has become hypercapnic has a ventilation problem, not a diffusion problem, and that distinction changes the management entirely.

Where each term becomes the clinical lever

TermClinical situation
Area, reducedEmphysema, lobectomy, pulmonary embolism
Thickness, increasedPulmonary oedema, fibrosis, ARDS
Gradient, reducedAltitude, low FIO2
Gradient, increasedSupplemental oxygen, the single most available intervention

Raising FIO2 works on the only term in Fick's law that you can change at the bedside within seconds. You cannot alter area, thickness, solubility, or molecular weight. You can always alter the gradient.

Why is nitrous oxide's speed of onset explained by Henry's law and solubility rather than by Graham's law?

Because the alveolar membrane is a membrane, not an orifice. Graham's law describes effusion through a hole, where nothing dissolves and only molecular velocity matters. Uptake into blood requires dissolving into tissue and plasma, which puts it squarely under Fick, where the solubility term dominates.

A patient has severe pulmonary fibrosis. Which blood gas abnormality appears first, and why?

Hypoxaemia. Thickening increases d for both gases equally, but oxygen has almost no solubility reserve to absorb the penalty while carbon dioxide has a twenty-fold margin. Oxygen transfer fails first, and by a wide margin.
