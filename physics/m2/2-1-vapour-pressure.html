2.1  Vapour Pressure and Saturated Vapour Pressure

One property, dependent on temperature and nothing else, from which the entire design of a vaporiser follows.

On finishing this section you should be able to
  • Explain vapour pressure in terms of the kinetic energy distribution of molecules at a liquid surface.
  • Define saturated vapour pressure and state the only variable it depends on.
  • Compute saturated vapour concentration from SVP and ambient pressure.
  • Rank the volatile agents by SVP and explain why desflurane is the outlier.

Where vapour pressure comes from

Molecules in a liquid do not all carry the same kinetic energy. Energy is distributed, and at any instant a minority of surface molecules carry enough to break free of intermolecular attraction and enter the gas phase.

Molecules escape the liquid surface and enter the space above it.
Some of those molecules collide back into the liquid and are recaptured.
As vapour accumulates, the recapture rate rises.
At some density, escape rate equals recapture rate. The system is at equilibrium.
The pressure exerted by the vapour at that equilibrium is the saturated vapour pressure.
The one fact this module is built on

Saturated vapour pressure depends on temperature and the identity of the substance. Nothing else.

It does not depend on ambient pressure. It does not depend on the volume of the container. It does not depend on how much liquid is present. Raising the temperature shifts the kinetic energy distribution rightward, more molecules clear the escape threshold, and SVP rises non-linearly.

Every apparently strange behaviour later in this course, the nitrous cylinder gauge from Module 1, altitude compensation in Module 4, and the entire existence of the desflurane vaporiser, is a direct consequence of that sentence.

The numbers

Published values vary by a few mmHg between sources. Learn the ranking and the order of magnitude rather than the third significant figure.

Saturated vapour concentration (%)  =  (SVP / Pambient) × 100 Sevoflurane: 157 / 760 = 20.7%. This is the maximum concentration obtainable from a fully saturated carrier stream, and it is the ceiling a vaporiser must dilute down from.

Why desflurane breaks the pattern

Desflurane's SVP of 669 mmHg is within 12% of one atmosphere at room temperature, and its boiling point of 22.8 °C sits below the temperature of many operating rooms. A conventional variable bypass vaporiser cannot handle this. The saturated stream is 88% agent, so the required dilution ratio becomes enormous and unstable, and on a warm day the liquid is boiling rather than evaporating.

Clinical anchor

This is why the desflurane vaporiser is a heated, pressurised, electronically controlled gas blender rather than a splitting-ratio device. It holds the agent at 39 °C and about 2 atmospheres, keeps it as a pressurised liquid, and injects measured vapour into the fresh gas stream. Module 4 covers the machine. Module 2 explains why the machine has to exist.

Discrimination trap

SVP is independent of ambient pressure. Boiling point is not. Boiling point is the temperature at which SVP happens to equal ambient pressure, so it falls as you climb. Section 2.2 is entirely this distinction.

Two sealed containers of sevoflurane at 20 °C, one holding 5 mL of liquid and one holding 200 mL. Which has the higher vapour pressure above the liquid?

Neither. Both read 157 mmHg. SVP is an equilibrium property of the substance at that temperature and is completely independent of the quantity of liquid present. This is the same reasoning that makes a nitrous cylinder gauge useless for estimating contents.

Why does SVP rise non-linearly with temperature rather than proportionally?

Because the fraction of molecules exceeding the escape energy follows a Boltzmann distribution, which is exponential in temperature. A small temperature rise moves a disproportionately large number of molecules past the threshold, so the SVP curve steepens as it climbs.

Doubling the volume of liquid agent in a vaporiser sump doubles the saturated vapour pressure above it.

Which agent has the highest saturated vapour pressure at 20 °C?
MechanismDesflurane at 669 mmHg, against 243 for halothane, 238 for isoflurane, and 157 for sevoflurane. Its weak intermolecular forces let far more molecules escape at a given temperature.
Q6
Saturated vapour pressure of sevoflurane at 20 °C, in mmHg.
MechanismAbout 157 mmHg, giving a saturated vapour concentration of roughly 21%. Sources cite 157 to 160 depending on the reference temperature used.
Q7
Give the equation for saturated vapour concentration as a percentage.
MechanismThe vapour's partial pressure divided by total pressure gives its fractional concentration, by Dalton's law. This is the ceiling a variable bypass vaporiser must dilute down from.
