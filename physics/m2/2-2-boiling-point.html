2.2  Boiling Point and Its Pressure Dependence

Boiling point is not a property of a substance. It is a property of a substance at a stated pressure.

On finishing this section you should be able to
  • Define boiling point in terms of saturated vapour pressure and ambient pressure.
  • Predict the direction and rough magnitude of boiling point change with altitude.
  • Explain why desflurane is uniquely vulnerable to ambient pressure change.
  • Relate boiling point to the phase diagram and the critical point from Module 1.

The definition that makes it make sense

A liquid boils when its saturated vapour pressure equals the pressure of the atmosphere pressing down on it. Below that temperature, vapour forming inside the liquid is crushed by ambient pressure and cannot sustain a bubble. At that temperature, bubbles can form throughout the bulk rather than only at the surface, and that is what boiling is.

Boiling occurs when   SVP(T)  =  Pambient Because SVP depends only on temperature and Pambient is external, boiling point moves whenever ambient pressure moves.
SVP rises with temperature along a fixed curve for each substance.
Ambient pressure sets a horizontal line across that curve.
Boiling point is simply where the curve crosses the line.
Lower the line, by going to altitude, and the crossing point moves left. The liquid boils cooler.
Clinical anchor

Water boils at 100 °C at sea level and around 90 °C in Denver, which is why autoclave sterilisation is pressurised rather than merely hot. Raising the pressure raises the boiling point, letting steam reach 121 °C and actually kill spores. The same physics, run in the opposite direction.

Why desflurane is the agent that cares

Desflurane boils at 22.8 °C at one atmosphere. That is inside the range of ordinary room temperature, and it means the margin between "volatile liquid" and "boiling liquid" is a few degrees rather than a few tens of degrees.

Take that bottle to a facility at altitude, where ambient pressure is lower, and desflurane's boiling point falls further. It can boil in the bottle. The engineering answer is to stop depending on ambient pressure at all: the desflurane vaporiser holds its own internal pressure at roughly 2 atmospheres and its own temperature at 39 °C, so the agent inside sits at a controlled point on its vapour pressure curve regardless of what the room is doing.

Discrimination trap

Both of these are true and they sound contradictory until you separate the variables:

  • Saturated vapour pressure is unaffected by altitude, because it depends only on temperature.
  • Boiling point falls at altitude, because ambient pressure has dropped to meet an unchanged SVP curve at a lower temperature.

Nothing about the liquid changed. The reference line moved.

A sealed sevoflurane bottle is carried to 3,000 m. Does the liquid inside boil?

No, and for two reasons. The bottle is sealed, so the pressure inside is set by the agent's own SVP and is not tracking the outside. And even in an open container, sevoflurane's boiling point at that altitude is still far above room temperature because its sea-level boiling point of 58.6 °C leaves an enormous margin. Desflurane, with 1.8 °C of margin, is the agent where this question has a different answer.

Connect boiling point to critical temperature from Module 1.

They are two points on the same phase diagram. The vapour pressure curve runs from the triple point up to the critical point, and boiling point is the temperature at which that curve crosses your chosen ambient pressure. The curve simply ends at the critical point, beyond which liquid and gas are indistinguishable and no boiling transition exists at all.
