2.6  Blood:Gas, Oil:Gas, and Brain:Blood Partition Coefficients

Three numbers per agent. From them you can predict speed of onset, potency, and duration without knowing anything else about the drug.

On finishing this section you should be able to
  • Explain why a LOW blood:gas coefficient produces FAST induction.
  • Relate the oil:gas coefficient to potency via the Meyer-Overton correlation.
  • Use brain:blood and fat:blood coefficients to predict equilibration and emergence.
  • Rank the volatile agents on all three coefficients from memory.

Blood:gas and the counterintuitive result

The correct statement is that a low blood:gas coefficient produces a fast induction. Almost everyone gets this backwards on first encounter, because "more soluble" sounds like "gets into the patient faster." Here is why it is the reverse.

The brain does not respond to how much agent is in the blood. It responds to partial pressure.
Alveolar partial pressure is what drives agent into blood, and brain partial pressure chases alveolar partial pressure.
A highly soluble agent is greedily taken up by blood, which drains the alveolus and holds alveolar partial pressure down.
Partial pressure cannot rise until blood is close to saturated, and a large capacity takes a long time to fill.
A poorly soluble agent saturates blood almost immediately, so alveolar partial pressure rises fast toward inspired.
Low solubility therefore means fast onset, and fast offset by exactly the same argument run backwards.
The sink analogy, stated carefully

Think of blood as a reservoir being filled, where partial pressure is the level and solubility is the cross-sectional area. A wide reservoir, meaning high solubility, takes a large volume of agent before the level rises appreciably. A narrow one comes up to level almost immediately. The brain reads the level, not the volume delivered. That is the whole mechanism.

Oil:gas and potency

The Meyer-Overton correlation is the observation that anesthetic potency tracks lipid solubility across an enormous range of chemically unrelated compounds. Higher oil:gas means more potent, which means lower MAC.

oil:gas  ×  MAC  ≈  constant (roughly 150) Desflurane: 19 × 6.0 = 114. Sevoflurane: 47 × 2.0 = 94. Isoflurane: 91 × 1.15 = 105. Halothane: 224 × 0.75 = 168. Nitrous: 1.4 × 104 = 146. The correlation is strong but not exact, and its exceptions are what eventually undermined the lipid theory of anesthetic action.
Discrimination trap

Two different coefficients, two different properties, and they are routinely confused:

  • Blood:gas predicts speed. Low means fast.
  • Oil:gas predicts potency. High means potent, so MAC is low.

Desflurane makes the distinction concrete: lowest blood:gas of the volatiles, so fastest, and low oil:gas, so weakest. Fast and weak are not contradictory because they are governed by different numbers.

Brain:blood and fat:blood

Brain:blood governs how quickly the brain equilibrates once blood partial pressure has risen. It is close to 1 to 3 for all agents, so it modifies the picture rather than driving it.

Fat:blood is where the long cases live. Fat coefficients are very large, roughly 27 for sevoflurane, 45 for isoflurane, and 51 for halothane, and adipose perfusion is poor. Fat therefore fills slowly and empties slowly. In a short case it is almost irrelevant. In a six-hour case it becomes a substantial reservoir that keeps releasing agent long after the vaporiser is off.

Clinical anchor

This is why emergence times converge on short cases and diverge on long ones. For a 30-minute case the difference between sevoflurane and isoflurane is modest. For a six-hour case in an obese patient it is large, and it is the reason desflurane retains a niche in prolonged bariatric surgery despite its cost and its airway irritation.

Nitrous oxide has a blood:gas coefficient of 0.47 and an oil:gas of 1.4. Predict its clinical profile.

Very fast on and off, because blood:gas is low and second only to desflurane. And very weak, because oil:gas is tiny, giving a MAC of 104% that cannot be reached at one atmosphere. Fast and unable to produce anesthesia alone. Both facts read directly off the two numbers.

Two agents have identical blood:gas coefficients but oil:gas values differing tenfold. What differs clinically?

Onset and offset speed would be essentially identical, since that is governed by blood:gas. Potency would differ roughly tenfold, so their MAC values differ by about the same factor and you would dial very different percentages to reach the same anesthetic depth.
