1.3  Boyle, Charles, and Gay-Lussac

Three special cases of one equation, and the only three you will ever be asked to apply.

On finishing this section you should be able to
  • State each of the three gas laws and identify which variable is held constant.
  • Collapse all three into the combined gas law and the ideal gas equation.
  • Apply Boyle's law to cylinder contents estimation.
  • Apply Gay-Lussac's law to predict cylinder pressure change with temperature.
  • Explain why absolute temperature is mandatory in all three.
What you already have

PV = nRT, and the kinetic theory behind it. This section is entirely recall for you. The only thing worth slowing down for is the clinical arithmetic, because the exam questions are applied and the errors are almost always unit errors rather than conceptual ones.

Three laws, one equation

LawRelationshipHeld constantClinical use
BoyleP ∝ 1/VTemperatureCylinder contents, lung mechanics, plethysmography
CharlesV ∝ TPressureGas volume correction, BTPS conversion
Gay-LussacP ∝ TVolumeCylinder pressure change with ambient temperature
P1V1 / T1  =  P2V2 / T2 The combined gas law. Hold any one variable constant and it degenerates into one of the three above. Add the mole term and it becomes PV = nRT.

Temperature must be absolute

All three laws are proportionalities in absolute temperature. Kelvin, not Celsius. The reason is structural: a proportionality demands that zero on the scale means zero of the quantity, and 0 °C is an arbitrary point on a water phase diagram, not an absence of thermal energy.

Discrimination trap

Using Celsius in a Gay-Lussac calculation is the single most common arithmetic error in this material, and it produces answers that are wrong by a wide and non-obvious margin. Convert first, always: K = °C + 273.

Boyle applied: what is left in the cylinder

An oxygen cylinder is a fixed volume of true gas above its critical temperature, so contents are directly proportional to gauge pressure.

A full E-cylinder holds about 660 L at about 2000 psi.
Contents scale linearly: at 1000 psi it holds about 330 L.
Divide remaining volume by flow rate to get time to empty.
Subtract a working reserve, because the last portion of a cylinder is not reliably usable at pressure.
Clinical anchor

Transporting a ventilated patient on an E-cylinder reading 1500 psi at 6 L/min gives you (1500/2000) × 660 = 495 L, so 495 / 6 ≈ 82 minutes. That calculation is worth being able to do in your head, because the situations that demand it are the ones where you have no time to do it on paper.

Gay-Lussac applied: the cylinder that got warm

Cylinder volume is fixed, so pressure tracks absolute temperature directly.

P1 / T1  =  P2 / T2 A cylinder reading 2000 psi at 20 °C (293 K), moved into a 35 °C (308 K) environment, rises to 2000 × 308/293 ≈ 2102 psi. Nothing was added. The molecules simply hit the walls harder.
Why does the same reasoning fail for a nitrous oxide cylinder?

Because nitrous contains liquid. Its pressure is set by the saturated vapour pressure of the liquid at that temperature, not by the ideal gas law applied to a fixed number of gas molecules. Warming a nitrous cylinder does raise its pressure, but through the vapour pressure curve rather than through Gay-Lussac, and the rise is much steeper.

Why is the ideal gas law an approximation, and where does it break down clinically?

It assumes point molecules with no intermolecular forces. Real gases deviate as they approach condensation, which is to say at high pressure and low temperature. That is exactly the regime inside a cylinder, which is why cylinder contents are quoted from measurement rather than calculated from PV = nRT, and why the Joule-Thomson effect in section 1.6 exists at all.

Q1
An oxygen E-cylinder reads 1500 psi. A full cylinder holds 660 L at 2000 psi. At 6 L/min, time to empty is approximately:
MechanismContents scale linearly with pressure for a true gas: (1500/2000) × 660 = 495 L. Dividing by 6 L/min gives 82.5 minutes.
Q2
A cylinder reading 1800 psi at 17 °C is moved to a room at 42 °C. The new pressure is closest to:
MechanismGay-Lussac in Kelvin: 290 K → 315 K. 1800 × 315/290 = 1955 psi. Using Celsius directly gives a wildly wrong answer, which is the point of the question.
Q3
Which law describes the pressure change in a rigid cylinder as ambient temperature rises?
MechanismVolume is fixed by the cylinder wall, so pressure varies directly with absolute temperature. Boyle holds temperature constant and Charles holds pressure constant, so neither applies.
Q4
Gas law calculations may be performed using degrees Celsius provided both temperatures use the same scale.
MechanismThese are proportionalities in absolute temperature. Using Celsius makes the ratio meaningless because zero on that scale does not represent zero thermal energy.
Q5
Boyle's law is applied clinically in all of the following except:
MechanismCylinder pressure rise with temperature at fixed volume is Gay-Lussac. The other three all hold temperature roughly constant and trade pressure against volume.
Q6
Convert 27 °C to kelvin.
MechanismK = °C + 273. Absolute temperature is required because the gas laws are proportionalities, and a proportionality needs a scale whose zero means none of the quantity.
Q7
Approximate volume in litres of a full oxygen E-cylinder.
MechanismAbout 660 L at roughly 2000 psi. Contents fall linearly with gauge pressure because oxygen at room temperature is above its critical temperature and behaves as a true gas.
