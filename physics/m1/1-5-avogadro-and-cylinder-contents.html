1.5  Avogadro's Law and Cylinder Contents

Turning a mass on a scale into a volume of gas, which is the only way to audit a nitrous cylinder.

On finishing this section you should be able to
  • State Avogadro's law and the molar volume of an ideal gas at STP.
  • Convert cylinder mass to delivered gas volume for nitrous oxide.
  • Explain why this calculation is necessary for nitrous and unnecessary for oxygen.
  • Compute time to empty from contents and flow rate.

The law

Equal volumes of any gases, at the same temperature and pressure, contain equal numbers of molecules. One mole of any ideal gas occupies 22.4 L at standard temperature and pressure, and about 24 L at 20 °C and one atmosphere.

What you already have

Molar arithmetic. The only new content here is the clinical procedure it enables and the specific constants for nitrous oxide.

Why this matters only for nitrous

Oxygen is a true gas at room temperature, so its gauge pressure is a direct proxy for contents and no mass measurement is needed. Nitrous is stored as a liquid at a pressure fixed by its vapour pressure, so the gauge carries no contents information at all. Mass is the only remaining handle.

Weigh the cylinder.
Subtract the tare weight stamped on the cylinder shoulder. The difference is the mass of nitrous.
Divide by the molar mass of N2O, which is 44 g/mol.
Multiply by molar volume, 22.4 L/mol at STP.
The result is the volume of gas that cylinder will deliver.
V  =  (mass in grams / 44)  ×  22.4 A full nitrous E-cylinder holds about 3.4 kg of liquid. (3400 / 44) × 22.4 = 1731 L, which is why the nominal figure is quoted as roughly 1590–1730 L depending on the source and reference conditions.
Clinical anchor

The nitrous E-cylinder carries more than twice the gas volume of the oxygen E-cylinder despite reading a quarter of the pressure. If that seems backwards, it is because pressure and quantity are only linked for a true gas. Liquid storage packs vastly more molecules into the same cylinder volume.

Time to empty

t  =  Vremaining / flow rate Trivial arithmetic, and the reason for every calculation above it.
Why does molar volume differ between 22.4 L and 24 L depending on the source?

Reference conditions. 22.4 L/mol is at standard temperature and pressure, meaning 0 °C and 1 atm. At 20 °C the same mole expands to about 24 L by Charles' law. Exam questions almost always intend 22.4, but the discrepancy is worth recognising rather than treating as an error.

Q1
A nitrous oxide cylinder weighs 8.9 kg. Its stamped tare weight is 5.5 kg. Approximate deliverable volume is:
MechanismContents are 3.4 kg. (3400 g / 44 g·mol⁻¹) × 22.4 L·mol⁻¹ = 1731 L. The 660 L distractor is the oxygen cylinder figure.
Q2
Cylinder contents must be determined by weight for nitrous oxide because:
MechanismWhile liquid remains, the vapour above it sits at its saturated vapour pressure, which depends only on temperature. The gauge therefore reads the same whether the cylinder is nearly full or nearly empty.
Q3
One mole of an ideal gas at standard temperature and pressure occupies:
Mechanism22.4 L/mol at 0 °C and 1 atm, from Avogadro's law. The 44 distractor is the molar mass of nitrous oxide in grams, not a volume.
Q4
A nitrous oxide E-cylinder holds a greater volume of gas than an oxygen E-cylinder despite a lower gauge pressure.
MechanismNitrous is stored as a liquid, which is far denser than compressed gas, so the same cylinder volume contains many more molecules despite the lower pressure reading.
Q5
Molar mass of nitrous oxide, in grams per mole.
MechanismTwo nitrogen at 14 plus one oxygen at 16 gives 44 g/mol. This is the divisor that converts cylinder mass into moles before applying molar volume.
