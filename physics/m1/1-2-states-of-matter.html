1.2  States of Matter, Critical Temperature, Critical Pressure

The single distinction that explains why an oxygen gauge tells the truth and a nitrous gauge does not.

On finishing this section you should be able to
  • Define critical temperature and critical pressure.
  • Distinguish a gas from a vapour using critical temperature.
  • Explain why oxygen cylinder pressure is proportional to contents and nitrous oxide cylinder pressure is not.
  • State the filling ratio and explain the hazard it prevents.
  • Explain pseudocritical temperature and the risk it creates in Entonox.

This section contains one idea. Everything downstream about cylinders, vaporisers, and gas storage falls out of it.

The idea

Critical temperature is the temperature above which a substance cannot be liquefied by pressure alone, no matter how much pressure you apply. Critical pressure is the pressure required to liquefy it at exactly that temperature.

Above its critical temperature a substance is a gas. Below it, the same substance is a vapour, meaning it can coexist as liquid and vapour in the same container. That is the entire distinction, and in ordinary speech the two words are used interchangeably, which is why the concept is routinely mangled.

Why a critical point exists at all

Below the critical temperature, liquid and gas are physically distinct phases with different densities and a visible meniscus between them. Squeezing the gas eventually forces molecules close enough that attractive forces dominate and it condenses. Above the critical temperature the molecules carry enough kinetic energy that no amount of compression lets attraction win. Density becomes continuous, the meniscus disappears, and there is no longer a meaningful difference between the phases.

Nitrous oxide at 20 °C is below its critical temperature of 36.5 °C.
So it liquefies under pressure and sits in the cylinder as liquid with vapour above it.
The vapour above a liquid is at its saturated vapour pressure, which depends on temperature and not on how much liquid is present.
Drawing gas off evaporates more liquid, which restores the same vapour pressure.
The gauge therefore reads roughly 745 psi whether the cylinder is 90% full or 10% full.
Only once the last liquid evaporates does the pressure begin to fall, and at that point the cylinder is nearly empty.
Clinical anchor

You cannot determine remaining nitrous oxide from the gauge. The only reliable method is weighing the cylinder and subtracting the tare weight stamped on the shoulder. A gauge that has just started to fall is not a cylinder that is half empty, it is a cylinder that is nearly done.

Filling ratio

Because nitrous is stored as a liquid, a cylinder filled completely would have no vapour space, and any warming would expand the liquid against an incompressible boundary and produce a catastrophic pressure rise. The filling ratio is the mass of fluid in the cylinder divided by the mass of water that would fill it entirely.

ClimateFilling ratio
Temperate regions0.75
Tropical regions0.67

The tropical ratio is lower precisely because higher ambient temperature expands the liquid further and demands more headspace.

Pseudocritical temperature

Entonox is a 50:50 premix of nitrous oxide and oxygen. Mixed together the two behave as a single gas down to a pseudocritical temperature of about −5.5 °C at 117 bar. Below that the nitrous separates out as a liquid at the bottom of the cylinder.

Discrimination trap

A separated Entonox cylinder does not simply stop working. It delivers an oxygen-rich mixture first, then a dangerously hypoxic nitrous-rich mixture at the end. That ordering is the exam question. The remedy is to store cylinders above 10 °C and invert them repeatedly before use.

Oxygen is stored at 2000 psi and nitrous at 745 psi, yet the nitrous cylinder holds more than twice the volume of gas. Why?

Because nitrous is stored as a liquid. Liquids are far denser than compressed gases, so a given cylinder volume holds far more molecules. The lower gauge pressure reflects the saturated vapour pressure of nitrous at room temperature, not a smaller quantity.

Would a nitrous cylinder behave like an oxygen cylinder in an operating room held at 40 °C?

Yes. 40 °C is above the critical temperature of 36.5 °C, so the nitrous could not exist as a liquid and would be present only as a gas. Its gauge pressure would then fall progressively with contents, exactly as oxygen does.
