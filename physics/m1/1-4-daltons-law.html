1.4  Dalton's Law of Partial Pressures

The most-used concept in anesthesia, and the one most often stated loosely.

On finishing this section you should be able to
  • State Dalton's law and compute a partial pressure from fractional concentration and total pressure.
  • Explain why partial pressure, not concentration, drives diffusion and clinical effect.
  • Predict the effect of altitude on inspired oxygen partial pressure at constant FiO2.
  • Explain why MAC is defined at one atmosphere.
  • Distinguish the altitude behaviour of a variable bypass vaporiser from a gas-blending vaporiser.

If you retain one thing from Module 1, retain this. Partial pressure is the currency of every gas exchange, every anesthetic depth, and every diffusion gradient you will ever manage.

The law

In a mixture of gases, each gas exerts the pressure it would exert if it occupied the container alone, and the total pressure is the sum of these partial pressures.

Ptotal = P1 + P2 + P3 …   and   Pgas = Fgas × Ptotal F is the fractional concentration. Room air at sea level: FO2 = 0.21, so PO2 = 0.21 × 760 = 160 mmHg.

Why concentration is the wrong variable

Molecules diffuse down a partial pressure gradient, not a concentration gradient. This distinction is invisible at sea level, where the two track each other exactly, and becomes decisive the moment total pressure changes.

A patient breathes 21% oxygen at sea level. PO2 = 0.21 × 760 = 160 mmHg.
The same patient breathes 21% oxygen at 4,000 m, where ambient pressure is about 460 mmHg.
PO2 = 0.21 × 460 = 97 mmHg. The concentration has not changed at all.
The driving gradient into blood has fallen by 40%, and the patient is hypoxic.
The correction everyone forgets

Inspired gas is humidified to saturation in the airway, and water vapour contributes its own partial pressure of 47 mmHg at 37 °C, which is temperature-dependent and pressure-independent. Subtract it before applying the fraction:

PIO2 = FIO2 × (Patm − 47)

At sea level on room air: 0.21 × (760 − 47) = 150 mmHg, not 160. That 47 mmHg is a fixed subtraction, so its proportional impact grows as ambient pressure falls, which makes altitude worse than the naive calculation suggests.

Clinical anchor

MAC is defined as a percentage at one atmosphere, but the brain responds to partial pressure. A MAC of 2% sevoflurane means 0.02 × 760 = 15.2 mmHg of anesthetic in the alveolus. Quote MAC as a percentage in Denver and you are describing a different pharmacological state than the same percentage at sea level. This returns as a full section in Module 9.

The vaporiser consequence

This is the classic altitude question and it separates the two vaporiser designs you will meet in Module 4.

Variable bypass (sevo, iso)Gas blender (desflurane)
Output at altitudeHigher volume %Unchanged volume %
Partial pressure deliveredRoughly preservedFalls with ambient pressure
Clinical effectApproximately maintainedUnder-dosed

The reason is that saturated vapour pressure depends on temperature, not on ambient pressure. A variable bypass vaporiser therefore adds the same absolute quantity of vapour regardless of altitude, and since total pressure has fallen, that same quantity now represents a larger fraction. The partial pressure delivered is roughly unchanged, which is what the patient responds to. A desflurane vaporiser, which blends to a set percentage electronically, has no such self-correction.

Discrimination trap

The intuitive answer is that a vaporiser under-doses at altitude. For a variable bypass vaporiser that is wrong, and the mechanism above is why. The device that genuinely under-doses at altitude is the desflurane vaporiser, because it delivers a set percentage rather than a set vapour quantity.

Why does the 47 mmHg water vapour term not scale with altitude?

Because saturated vapour pressure is a function of temperature alone. Body temperature does not change with altitude, so the water vapour contribution stays at 47 mmHg while everything around it shrinks. Its proportional bite therefore grows as ambient pressure falls.

A patient on 100% oxygen at sea level. What is the inspired partial pressure?

1.00 × (760 − 47) = 713 mmHg. Not 760. Humidification takes its share regardless of what fraction you are delivering.
