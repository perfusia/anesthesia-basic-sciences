1.1  SI Units and Dimensional Analysis in Anesthesia

Why five different pressure units are in use in the same operating room, and which one belongs where.

On finishing this section you should be able to
  • State the SI definition of pressure and derive the pascal from base units.
  • Convert freely between mmHg, cmH2O, kPa, psi, bar, and atm.
  • Explain why mercury and water manometry differ by a factor of 13.6.
  • Distinguish gauge pressure from absolute pressure and say which one a cylinder gauge reports.
  • Predict which unit will be used for a given clinical measurement and justify it.

Anesthesia is the only clinical setting where you will routinely read five different pressure units within one metre of each other. Arterial line in mmHg. Airway pressure in cmH2O. Cylinder in psi. Gas analyser in kPa. None of this is arbitrary, and once you see why each unit survived, you stop needing a conversion table.

Pressure is force per unit area

That is the whole definition. Everything else is a unit choice.

P = F / A F in newtons (kg·m·s−2), A in m². One newton over one square metre is one pascal. The pascal is tiny, which is the root of the entire mess below.
What you already have Dimensional analysis and SI base units. Nothing in this section is a new capability. What is new is the set of historical constants and the clinical convention for where each unit is used. Treat it as vocabulary, not physics.

Why manometry left us two units

Before transducers, you measured a pressure by letting it push a liquid column up a tube and reading the height. The unit became the height of the column, which means the unit silently encodes the density of whatever liquid was in it.

A column of liquid exerts pressure P = ρgh, where ρ is density, g is gravity, h is height.
Mercury has a density of 13,600 kg/m³. Water is 1,000 kg/m³.
For the same pressure, the water column must be 13.6 times taller than the mercury column.
So 1 mmHg = 13.6 mmH2O = 1.36 cmH2O.

That single ratio explains the whole convention. Arterial pressures are large, so mercury keeps the column short enough to fit on a wall. Airway pressures are small, so water gives you finer resolution across the clinically useful range. Neither unit is more correct. They are two different liquids in two different tubes.

Clinical anchor

Pipeline gas arrives at roughly 50–55 psi, and every anesthesia machine is designed around that number. A full oxygen E-cylinder sits near 2000 psi, which is why the first-stage regulator drops it to around 45 psi so the pipeline always wins if both are connected. PEEP of 5 cmH2O is about 3.7 mmHg, which is worth internalising the moment you start comparing airway pressure to venous pressure across the same alveolar capillary.

Gauge versus absolute

A cylinder gauge reads gauge pressure: pressure relative to ambient. It reads zero when the cylinder is at atmospheric pressure, not when it is at true vacuum. Absolute pressure is gauge plus one atmosphere.

Pabsolute = Pgauge + Patmospheric

For cylinder arithmetic at 2000 psi the distinction is a rounding error. For anything near atmospheric it is not, which is why blood gas partial pressures and alveolar gas equations are always absolute.

Discrimination trap

An E-cylinder gauge reading zero does not contain no gas. It contains gas at ambient pressure. This matters for scavenging and for any calculation that treats the gauge number as a proportion of contents.

Why is central venous pressure sometimes quoted in cmH2O and sometimes in mmHg?

Historical transducer choice. Water manometers were the original bedside method and gave better resolution at the low pressures involved. Electronic transducers report in mmHg by convention because they are shared with arterial monitoring. A CVP of 8 mmHg is roughly 11 cmH2O, so failing to note the unit produces a 36% error in the direction of thinking the patient is drier than they are.

Derive the pascal from SI base units without looking it up.

Pressure is force over area. Force is mass times acceleration, so kg·m·s−2. Divide by m²: kg·m−1·s−2. That is one pascal.

Q2
The factor of 13.6 relating mmHg to cmH2O originates in:
MechanismColumn pressure is ρgh. Mercury's density is 13,600 kg/m³ against water's 1,000, so for equal pressure the water column stands 13.6 times taller.
Q3
Pipeline oxygen is delivered to the anesthesia machine at approximately:
MechanismPipeline supply runs near 50–55 psi. Cylinder first-stage regulators drop to about 45 psi deliberately, so that when both sources are open the pipeline supplies the machine and the cylinder is held in reserve.
Q4
A cylinder pressure gauge reading zero indicates the cylinder contains no gas at all.
MechanismGauges read pressure relative to ambient. Zero gauge means the contents are at atmospheric pressure, so the cylinder still holds a full volume of gas at 1 atm.

Which measurement would conventionally be reported in kilopascals?
MechanismkPa is the SI unit and is standard for blood gas partial pressures in most countries outside the US. Cylinders use psi or bar, airway pressure uses cmH2O, and US arterial pressure uses mmHg.
