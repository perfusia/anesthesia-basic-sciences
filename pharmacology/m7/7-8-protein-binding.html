7.8  Protein Binding

Only free drug does anything. The clinical significance of binding changes is real, smaller than usually taught, and concentrated in a specific set of drugs.

On finishing this section you should be able to
  • State which drug fraction is pharmacologically active.
  • Identify the principal binding proteins for acidic and basic drugs.
  • Predict the direction of binding protein changes in common clinical states.
  • Explain why altered binding rarely changes steady-state free concentration.

Only free drug acts

Bound drug is pharmacologically inert. It cannot cross membranes, reach receptors, be filtered at the glomerulus, or be metabolised. It functions as a circulating reservoir in rapid equilibrium with the free fraction.

They move in opposite directions in inflammation, which means acidic and basic drugs are affected oppositely by the same clinical event. Surgery lowers albumin and raises α₁-acid glycoprotein, so free phenytoin tends to rise while free local anaesthetic tends to fall.

The correction that most teaching omits

The standard teaching is that reduced binding produces more free drug and therefore more effect. For a single dose that is correct. At steady state it is usually wrong, and the reason is worth following.

  1. Reduced binding raises the free fraction.
  2. Only free drug is available for clearance, so clearance of the drug also rises.
  3. More is eliminated until free concentration returns close to where it began.
  4. Total concentration falls, while free concentration is largely restored.

So a hypoalbuminaemic patient on phenytoin shows a low total level with a normal free level. Increasing the dose in response to the low total number is a well-documented route to toxicity. Measure free phenytoin, or use a correction formula.

Where binding genuinely matters

The steady-state argument has exceptions, and they are the situations that hurt people:

  • Acute bolus dosing, before clearance has had time to compensate. A single dose of thiopentone in a hypoalbuminaemic patient produces a genuinely larger free peak.
  • Narrow therapeutic index drugs where a transient rise in free concentration is enough to cause harm.
  • High-extraction drugs given intravenously, where clearance is flow-limited and cannot rise in response to a higher free fraction.
  • Pregnancy and local anaesthetics. α₁-acid glycoprotein falls, raising the free fraction of bupivacaine, which contributes to the increased cardiotoxicity risk in obstetric practice.
A malnourished patient's total phenytoin is 6 mg/L, below the usual 10 to 20 range. Should the dose increase?

Probably not. Hypoalbuminaemia reduces bound drug, so the total measurement falls while the free, active concentration may be entirely therapeutic. Measure a free level or apply an albumin correction. Increasing the dose on the total value is a recognised cause of phenytoin toxicity.

Why does displacement of one drug by another rarely cause the toxicity textbooks warn about?

Because displacement raises the free fraction, which raises clearance of the free drug, and the system re-equilibrates at a similar free concentration with a lower total. The classic warfarin displacement interactions turn out to be mediated mainly by enzyme inhibition rather than by displacement itself. Displacement matters most acutely, before clearance can respond, and for drugs whose clearance cannot rise.
