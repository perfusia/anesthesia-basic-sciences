7.3  Clearance and Extraction Ratio

The primary parameter. It decides maintenance dosing, and whether cardiac output or enzyme function is the thing that matters for a given drug.

On finishing this section you should be able to
  • Define clearance and state its units.
  • Express organ clearance as the product of blood flow and extraction ratio.
  • Distinguish flow-limited from capacity-limited elimination.
  • Predict the effect of reduced cardiac output or enzyme inhibition for each class.

The definition

Clearance is the volume of plasma completely cleared of drug per unit time. It is not an amount and not a rate of removal; it is a volume per time.

Rate of elimination  =  Cl × C Which is why a first-order drug's absolute elimination rate falls as concentration falls, while clearance itself stays constant.
Discrimination trap

Clearance is constant in first-order kinetics. The rate of elimination is not, because it is the product of that constant and a falling concentration. Statements like "elimination slows as the drug wears off" describe the rate, not the clearance, and conflating them makes zero-order kinetics incomprehensible later.

Organ clearance

Clorgan  =  Q × ER Q = blood flow to the organ · ER = extraction ratio = (Carterial − Cvenous) / Carterial

Extraction ratio is the fraction of drug removed in a single pass. It runs from 0, nothing removed, to 1, everything removed. Total clearance is the sum across organs: hepatic plus renal plus everything else.

Reference flows worth holding: hepatic blood flow around 1.5 L/min, renal blood flow around 1.1 L/min, glomerular filtration around 125 mL/min.

A high-extraction drug is removed almost completely on each pass through the liver.
The enzymes are therefore working well below capacity, and adding more enzyme changes nothing.
The only way to clear more is to deliver more, so clearance tracks blood flow.
A low-extraction drug leaves the liver largely intact, so the enzymes are the bottleneck.
Delivering more drug does not help; the enzymes are already the limiting step.
Clinical anchor

This predicts what will go wrong in a specific patient. In haemorrhagic shock or heart failure, hepatic blood flow falls, so propofol, fentanyl, and lidocaine clearances fall and those drugs accumulate. Enzyme inhibition would barely touch them.

Conversely, an erythromycin or fluconazole interaction meaningfully raises warfarin or phenytoin concentrations, because those are capacity-limited and the enzyme is the constraint. Reduced cardiac output would barely touch them.

Same organ, opposite vulnerabilities, and the extraction ratio is what tells you which patient you are looking at.

Why do high-extraction drugs have poor oral bioavailability?

Because the entire absorbed dose passes through the liver via the portal vein before reaching the systemic circulation, and a high extraction ratio means most of it is removed on that single pass. Morphine's oral bioavailability of roughly 25% is exactly this. Low-extraction drugs pass through largely intact and have high oral bioavailability.

A patient on a propofol infusion becomes profoundly hypotensive with a low cardiac output. What happens kinetically?

Propofol is high-extraction, so its clearance is proportional to hepatic blood flow. Falling cardiac output reduces hepatic delivery and therefore clearance, and the drug accumulates at an unchanged infusion rate. The effect is self-reinforcing, since accumulating propofol worsens the hypotension that is reducing its clearance.
