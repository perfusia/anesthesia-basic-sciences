7.5  The One-Compartment Model

One differential equation, one solution, and the relationship that ties clearance and volume together into a single rate constant.

On finishing this section you should be able to
  • Write and solve the differential equation for one-compartment first-order elimination.
  • Define the elimination rate constant and state its units.
  • Derive k = Cl/Vd and explain what it means physically.
  • Interpret a semi-logarithmic concentration-time plot.

The equation

Drug leaves the compartment at a rate proportional to how much is there.

dC/dt  =  −kC k = elimination rate constant, units of inverse time (hr−1, min−1). The negative sign is elimination.

Separate and integrate:

C(t)  =  C0 e−kt      ln C(t)  =  ln C0 − kt The second form is the first with logs taken. It is a straight line of slope −k, which is how k is measured in practice.
What you already have

This is the simplest possible first-order ODE and you have solved it in half a dozen contexts: radioactive decay, RC discharge, Newton's cooling. The pharmacology adds one thing worth pausing on, which is the physical interpretation of k below.

Where k comes from

The elimination rate constant is not a primary property. It falls out of the two parameters from 7.2 and 7.3.

Rate of elimination in amount per time is Cl × C.
Amount in the body is Vd × C.
The fractional rate of loss is (amount lost per time) / (amount present) = Cl·C / Vd·C.
The concentration cancels.
k = Cl / Vd.
k  =  Cl / Vd Physically: the fraction of the distribution volume cleared per unit time. A k of 0.1 hr−1 means 10% of the volume is cleared each hour.
Why the concentration cancels

That cancellation is the whole reason first-order systems have a constant rate constant and a constant half-life. Both the amount present and the amount removed scale with C, so their ratio does not. The moment a process saturates, the numerator stops scaling with C, the cancellation fails, and you are in zero-order territory with no constant to speak of.

Clinical anchor

Almost no anaesthetic drug is genuinely one-compartment. Propofol, fentanyl, and the volatiles all need three. The one-compartment model earns its place as the base case whose behaviour you must know cold, because a three-compartment model is three of these running in parallel with transfer terms, and every intuition you build here carries forward. Module 8 is this section with more indices.

Two drugs have identical clearances but one has ten times the volume of distribution. Compare their elimination rate constants.

k = Cl/Vd, so the drug with the larger volume has a tenfold smaller k and therefore a tenfold longer half-life, despite identical clearance. This is why half-life alone tells you nothing about how well the body is removing a drug. A long half-life can mean poor clearance or extensive distribution, and those are entirely different clinical situations.

What does an elimination rate constant of 0.35 hr⁻¹ mean in plain terms?

35% of the drug present is eliminated each hour, or equivalently, a volume equal to 35% of the volume of distribution is cleared each hour. Since 0.693/0.35 ≈ 2, the half-life is about two hours.

MechanismSeparating and integrating a first-order homogeneous ODE gives exponential decay. The linear form describes zero-order elimination instead.
Q2
The elimination rate constant equals:
Mechanismk = Cl/Vd, obtained by dividing the elimination rate Cl·C by the amount present Vd·C. The concentration cancels, which is why k is constant.
Q3
On a semi-log plot of concentration against time, the slope equals:
MechanismTaking logs gives ln C = ln C₀ − kt, a straight line whose slope is the negative elimination rate constant. This is how k is measured in practice.
Q4
Two drugs have equal clearance but drug A has four times the volume of distribution. Drug A's half-life is:
Mechanismk = Cl/Vd, so quadrupling Vd quarters k and quadruples half-life. Identical clearance with different half-lives is the standard demonstration that half-life is derived rather than primary.
Q5
The units of the elimination rate constant are inverse time.
Mechanismk is a fractional rate, so it carries units of hr⁻¹ or min⁻¹. It represents the fraction of the distribution volume cleared per unit time.
Q6
A drug has clearance 6 L/hr and volume of distribution 30 L. Give k in per hour.
Mechanismk = Cl/Vd = 6/30 = 0.2 hr⁻¹, meaning 20% of the distribution volume is cleared each hour. Half-life follows as 0.693/0.2 ≈ 3.5 hours.
