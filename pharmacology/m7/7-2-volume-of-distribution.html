7.2  Volume of Distribution

A volume that is not a volume, routinely exceeds the size of the patient, and tells you the loading dose.

On finishing this section you should be able to
  • Define apparent volume of distribution and explain why it is apparent.
  • Calculate Vd from dose and initial concentration.
  • Calculate a loading dose from Vd and target concentration.
  • Predict Vd from a drug's physicochemical properties.

The definition

Vd  =  Dose / C0 C0 = plasma concentration extrapolated back to time zero. Units of volume, typically L or L/kg.

Read it as the answer to a hypothetical: if the drug distributed evenly throughout the body at the concentration measured in plasma, what volume would be required to hold the dose given? That is why it is called the apparent volume of distribution. It is a proportionality constant with units of volume, not a space you could drain.

Why it can exceed body size

A drug leaves plasma and binds avidly to tissue.
Plasma concentration therefore falls very low.
Vd is dose divided by that low concentration.
A small denominator produces a large quotient.
Digoxin has a Vd near 500 L in a patient who contains about 42 L of water.

Nothing paradoxical has happened. Vd is large precisely because the drug is not in the plasma, and the number is an index of tissue sequestration rather than a physical space.

Clinical anchor: Vd sets the loading dose

This is the reason to care about the number.

Loading dose  =  Vd × Ctarget

Note what is absent: clearance. A loading dose fills a volume, so it depends on Vd alone. A maintenance dose replaces what is removed, so it depends on clearance alone. This is why a patient in renal failure needs the same loading dose and a reduced maintenance dose, and getting that backwards is a classic and consequential error.

Discrimination trap

Oedema, ascites, and fluid resuscitation expand Vd for hydrophilic drugs, which is why aminoglycoside and neuromuscular blocker loading doses often need to be larger in a fluid-overloaded septic patient, not smaller, despite the impulse to reduce everything in a sick patient. Their small Vd is the extracellular space, and that space has grown.

A patient in renal failure needs a drug cleared entirely by the kidney. How do loading and maintenance doses change?

Loading dose is unchanged, because it depends on Vd and the volume the drug distributes into has not changed. Maintenance dose must fall, because it depends on clearance and clearance has collapsed. Reducing the loading dose only delays reaching a therapeutic concentration without reducing the eventual accumulation.

Why does high plasma protein binding produce a small Vd while high tissue binding produces a large one?

Vd is dose over plasma concentration. Protein binding holds drug in plasma, raising measured plasma concentration and shrinking the quotient. Tissue binding pulls drug out of plasma, lowering plasma concentration and inflating it. The measurement is made in plasma, so anything that keeps drug there reduces Vd.

Q1
Volume of distribution is described as apparent because it:
MechanismIt is dose divided by plasma concentration, a proportionality constant with volume units. Extensive tissue binding lowers plasma concentration and can produce values far exceeding body volume.
Q2
A 500 mg dose produces an extrapolated initial concentration of 10 mg/L. Vd is:
MechanismVd = Dose/C₀ = 500 mg / 10 mg/L = 50 L. Slightly above total body water, indicating modest tissue distribution.
Q3
Loading dose is determined by:
MechanismA loading dose fills a volume, so LD = Vd × target concentration. Maintenance dose replaces what is eliminated and therefore depends on clearance.
Q4
A patient with severe renal impairment requires a renally cleared drug. The loading dose should be:
MechanismLoading dose depends on Vd, which renal failure has not altered. Only the maintenance dose needs reduction, because only clearance has fallen.
Q5
Which property predicts a large volume of distribution?
MechanismLipophilic drugs partition into tissue, lowering plasma concentration and inflating the calculated volume. The other three all retain drug in plasma and shrink it.
Q6
A drug confined entirely to plasma would have a volume of distribution of about 3 litres in a 70 kg adult.
MechanismPlasma volume is roughly 3 L. Heparin and warfarin approach this, being large or highly protein-bound and unable to leave the vascular space.
Q7
Write the equation for loading dose.
MechanismLD = Vd × target concentration. Clearance does not appear, which is why renal or hepatic failure changes the maintenance dose but not the loading dose.
