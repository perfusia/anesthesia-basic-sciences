7.1  What a Pharmacokinetic Model Actually Models

Compartments are not anatomy. They are the smallest number of exponentials that fit the data, and knowing that changes how you read every number downstream.

On finishing this section you should be able to
  • Distinguish pharmacokinetics from pharmacodynamics.
  • Explain what a compartment represents and what it does not.
  • State the assumptions of a linear pharmacokinetic model.
  • Identify volume of distribution and clearance as the two independent parameters.

What a compartment is

A compartment is not an organ, a tissue, or a fluid space. It is a mathematical fiction: a hypothetical volume within which drug is assumed to distribute instantaneously and uniformly.

Measure plasma concentration after a bolus at many time points.
Plot the natural log of concentration against time.
If the result is a straight line, one exponential describes the data, so one compartment suffices.
If it curves, the residuals reveal a second exponential, and a second compartment is added.
Compartments are added until the residuals are noise.
The honest framing

The number of compartments is a property of the data and the sampling schedule, not of the patient. Sample early enough and often enough and a two-compartment drug becomes three-compartment. Nothing about the patient changed. The model got better resolution.

This is worth internalising because it explains why published values for the same drug differ between studies, and why parameters are not physical constants in the way a boiling point is.

What you already have

This is curve fitting to a sum of exponentials, and the compartment count is a model selection problem. You have made this exact trade before, between fit and overparameterisation. The pharmacology adds no new mathematics, only a physiological story laid over the fitted terms.

The assumptions

  1. Instantaneous mixing within each compartment. False, but fast relative to the timescale of interest.
  2. First-order transfer between compartments and out of the body. Rate proportional to concentration.
  3. Linearity. Doubling the dose doubles every concentration at every time point. Fails when any process saturates (7.4).
  4. Constant parameters. Vd and Cl do not change during the period modelled. Frequently false in critical illness.

Two independent parameters, everything else derived

The structural point of this module

Volume of distribution and clearance are the two independent parameters. They are physiologically separate: one describes how widely a drug spreads, the other how fast the body removes it.

Half-life is derived from both. It is not a primary property of a drug, and treating it as one is the most common conceptual error in clinical pharmacology. A half-life can change because Vd changed, because Cl changed, or because both changed in opposite directions and one won. Those situations demand different responses, and the half-life alone cannot tell you which occurred.

Why do published values for a drug's volume of distribution vary between studies?

Because Vd is a fitted parameter, not a measured volume. It depends on the model chosen, the sampling schedule, the duration of sampling, and the population studied. A study that stops sampling at four hours will fit a smaller terminal volume than one that samples for twenty-four, because it never observes the slow compartment emptying.

If a compartment is not anatomical, why do textbooks describe compartment two as "muscle" and three as "fat"?

Because the fitted rate constants correlate loosely with tissue perfusion, so the fast peripheral compartment behaves roughly like well-perfused tissue and the slow one roughly like poorly perfused fat. It is a useful mnemonic and a false statement. No compartment maps onto a tissue you could dissect, and drugs that behave identically kinetically may distribute into entirely different tissues.
