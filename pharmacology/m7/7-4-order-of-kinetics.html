7.4  First-Order and Zero-Order Kinetics

A constant fraction against a constant amount, the saturation that separates them, and why half-life stops meaning anything on the wrong side of the line.

On finishing this section you should be able to
  • Distinguish first-order from zero-order elimination.
  • Relate both to the Michaelis-Menten equation as limiting cases.
  • Name drugs that show zero-order kinetics at clinical doses.
  • Explain why half-life is undefined for zero-order elimination.

What you already have

Both are one-line integrations. The pharmacological content is entirely in why a system would be one or the other, which is saturation, and in which drugs sit near the boundary at doses people actually give.

They are the same equation at two extremes

Enzymatic elimination follows Michaelis-Menten kinetics, and first- and zero-order are its two limiting cases.

Rate  =  Vmax · C / (Km + C) Vmax = maximum rate when enzyme is saturated · Km = concentration at half Vmax
When C is much less than Km, the denominator is approximately Km, a constant.
Rate ≈ (Vmax/Km) × C, which is proportional to concentration. First-order.
When C is much greater than Km, the denominator is approximately C.
Rate ≈ Vmax, independent of concentration. Zero-order.
Between the two, kinetics are mixed and behaviour is non-linear and unpredictable.

So a drug is not intrinsically first- or zero-order. It is first-order at concentrations below its Km and zero-order above. Most drugs at clinical doses sit far below Km, which is why first-order is the normal case.

Clinical anchor

Phenytoin is the standard example and the reason this matters. Because it saturates inside the therapeutic range, a small dose increase can produce a disproportionate and dangerous rise in concentration. Going from 300 mg to 400 mg daily may more than double the plasma level rather than raising it by a third. Dose adjustments must be small and levels must be checked.

The same logic explains why an intoxicated patient's alcohol level falls linearly rather than exponentially, and why you can predict a time to sobriety with a ruler but not with a half-life.

Discrimination trap

Half-life is undefined in zero-order kinetics. Since a constant amount is removed per unit time, the time to fall by half depends entirely on where you started. From 200 to 100 takes twice as long as from 100 to 50. Quoting a half-life for ethanol is a category error.

Why does the semi-log plot distinguish the two orders so cleanly?

Taking logs of an exponential gives a straight line, so first-order elimination linearises with slope −k. Zero-order decay is already linear on ordinary axes, so taking logs of it produces a curve. The two plots swap which one looks straight, and that swap is the diagnostic.

A patient's phenytoin level is 15 mg/L on 300 mg daily. Why not simply increase to 450 mg to reach 22?

Because phenytoin saturates within the therapeutic range, so the relationship between dose and steady-state concentration is not proportional. A 50% dose increase can produce a threefold or greater rise in level and precipitate toxicity. Increase in small increments, typically 25 to 50 mg, and recheck.
