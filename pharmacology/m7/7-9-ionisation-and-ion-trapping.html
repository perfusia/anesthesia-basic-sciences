7.9  pKa, Ionisation, and Ion Trapping

Only the unionised form crosses a membrane. One equation predicts how much of it there is, and it explains why a block fails in infected tissue.

On finishing this section you should be able to
  • Apply the Henderson-Hasselbalch equation to weak acids and weak bases.
  • Calculate the unionised fraction from pKa and pH.
  • Relate local anaesthetic pKa to speed of onset.
  • Explain ion trapping and predict where it occurs.

The rule

Lipid membranes are crossed by the unionised form. The ionised form is charged, hydrophilic, and effectively excluded. So the fraction unionised determines how readily a drug reaches its site of action.

Weak acid:   pH = pKa + log([A−] / [HA]) HA is unionised. Acids are unionised in acidic environments.
Weak base:   pH = pKa + log([B] / [BH+]) B is unionised. Bases are unionised in alkaline environments.

pKa is the pH at which the drug is exactly 50% ionised. It is a property of the molecule, not the environment.

The working formula

For a weak base, the percentage unionised at a given pH is:

% unionised  =  100 / (1 + 10(pKa − pH))

Worth committing, because every local anaesthetic and opioid question reduces to it.

Local anaesthetics: pKa predicts onset

All local anaesthetics are weak bases with pKa above physiological pH, so all are predominantly ionised at pH 7.4. The one closest to 7.4 has the largest unionised fraction and the fastest onset.

AgentpKa% unionised at pH 7.4Onset
Mepivacaine7.639%Fast
Lidocaine7.924%Fast
Bupivacaine8.117%Slow
Procaine8.93%Slow
The exception worth knowing

Chloroprocaine has a pKa of 8.7, which predicts slow onset, yet it is clinically among the fastest. The reason is concentration: it is used at 3%, and low systemic toxicity permits that. A small unionised fraction of a large total still delivers a large unionised amount. pKa predicts onset only at comparable concentrations.

Why blocks fail in infected tissue

Infected tissue is acidic, with pH perhaps 6.4 rather than 7.4.
Lidocaine is a weak base, so a lower pH shifts equilibrium toward the ionised form.
At pH 7.4 lidocaine is 24% unionised. At pH 6.4 it is about 3%.
Roughly eight times less drug is able to cross the nerve membrane.
The block is slow, weak, or absent.

Adding bicarbonate to a local anaesthetic solution works on the same equation in reverse, raising pH, raising the unionised fraction, and speeding onset.

Alfentanil's very rapid onset despite lower lipid solubility than fentanyl is largely this: nearly 90% of it is in the form that can cross a membrane, against 9% for fentanyl.

Ion trapping

Unionised drug crosses a membrane into a compartment at a different pH.
On arrival, the new pH shifts equilibrium toward the ionised form.
Ionised drug cannot cross back.
Drug accumulates on the far side.
Clinical anchor

Fetal ion trapping. A distressed fetus is acidotic. Local anaesthetic, a weak base, crosses the placenta unionised, becomes ionised in the more acidic fetal circulation, and is trapped. Concentrations accumulate in the fetus. This is why acidosis in the fetus raises the stakes on maternal local anaesthetic dosing.

Urinary trapping. Alkalinising urine ionises weak acids such as salicylate, trapping them in the tubule and preventing reabsorption. This is the rationale for urinary alkalinisation in salicylate overdose.

Why does adding sodium bicarbonate to lidocaine speed onset?

It raises the pH of the solution. Lidocaine is a weak base, so a higher pH shifts the equilibrium toward the unionised form, increasing the fraction able to cross the nerve membrane. The dose is unchanged; the proportion in the membrane-permeable form is not.

Would a weak acid be trapped in an acidotic fetus?

No, the opposite. Acids are unionised in acidic environments, so a weak acid arriving in acidotic fetal blood becomes more unionised and crosses back readily. Trapping requires the destination pH to favour the ionised form, which for a base means an acidic destination and for an acid means an alkaline one.
