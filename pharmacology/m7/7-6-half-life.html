7.6  Half-Life as a Derived Quantity

The most quoted number in pharmacology and the most misused, because it is a ratio of two things that move independently.

On finishing this section you should be able to
  • Derive half-life from the elimination rate constant.
  • Express half-life in terms of volume of distribution and clearance.
  • Explain why a change in half-life is ambiguous without knowing which parameter moved.
  • Apply the five-half-life rule to elimination and to attainment of steady state.

The derivation

Start from C = C₀e−kt.
Set C = C₀/2 and solve for t.
½ = e−kt½, so ln(½) = −kt½.
t½ = ln2 / k = 0.693 / k.
Substituting k = Cl/Vd from 7.5 gives the form that matters.
t½  =  0.693 × Vd / Cl Half-life rises with volume of distribution and falls with clearance. It is a ratio, not a property.
The central point of Module 7

A prolonged half-life is ambiguous. It means Vd went up, or Cl went down, or both moved. These demand opposite responses:

  • Clearance fell (renal or hepatic failure): the drug accumulates on repeated dosing. Reduce the maintenance dose.
  • Volume rose (oedema, third-spacing, obesity for lipophilic drugs): the drug is diluted, not retained. The loading dose may need to increase, and the maintenance dose is unchanged if clearance is intact.

Reading a long half-life and reflexively cutting the dose is right in one case and wrong in the other. The half-life alone cannot distinguish them.

The five-half-life rule, in both directions

Half-livesFraction eliminatedFraction of steady state reached
150%50%
275%75%
387.5%87.5%
493.8%93.8%
596.9%96.9%

The symmetry is not a coincidence. Washing out and filling up are the same exponential run in opposite directions, so the rule of roughly five half-lives applies equally to eliminating a drug and to reaching steady state on a constant infusion.

Time to steady state is independent of dose

Raising the infusion rate raises the steady-state concentration but does not shorten the time to reach it, because that time is set by the exponential's rate constant alone. If you need a therapeutic concentration sooner than five half-lives, the only answer is a loading dose. This is the clinical reason section 7.2 exists.

Clinical anchor

The rule also sets when to check a level. A trough drawn before four or five half-lives have elapsed is not a steady-state trough, and adjusting a dose on it will overshoot. For a drug with a 12-hour half-life, that is roughly 60 hours, which is why levels drawn on the morning after starting a drug are so often misleading.

A septic patient's aminoglycoside half-life doubles. What are the two possible causes and how do you tell them apart?

Either clearance halved, from acute kidney injury, or volume of distribution doubled, from fluid resuscitation into an expanded extracellular space. Check renal function. If creatinine clearance is intact, the volume expanded and you need a larger loading dose with an unchanged maintenance dose. If creatinine clearance has fallen, extend the dosing interval instead.

Why does doubling an infusion rate not halve the time to steady state?

Because the approach to steady state is exponential with a rate constant of k, which depends on Cl and Vd and not on the rate of administration. Doubling the rate doubles the plateau and reaches it on exactly the same schedule. Only a loading dose changes the timing.
