7.7  Bioavailability and Area Under the Curve

How much of what you gave actually arrived, why the liver takes a cut first, and which routes avoid it.

On finishing this section you should be able to
  • Define bioavailability and state its value for intravenous administration.
  • List the factors reducing oral bioavailability.
  • Relate area under the curve to dose and clearance.
  • Identify routes that bypass first-pass hepatic metabolism.

Definition

Bioavailability (F) is the fraction of an administered dose that reaches the systemic circulation chemically unchanged. Intravenous administration is F = 1 by definition, since the drug is placed directly into the systemic circulation.

F  =  (AUCoral / AUCIV) × (DoseIV / Doseoral) Comparing total exposure by each route, corrected for any difference in dose given.

What reduces it

Incomplete dissolution or absorption across gut mucosa.
Degradation by gastric acid or intestinal enzymes.
Efflux back into the lumen by P-glycoprotein.
Metabolism in the gut wall itself, principally CYP3A4.
First-pass hepatic extraction, since portal blood traverses the liver before reaching the systemic circulation.
First-pass and extraction ratio are the same idea

A drug with a hepatic extraction ratio of 0.7 loses 70% of the absorbed dose on its first pass, so F cannot exceed 0.3 even with perfect absorption. High extraction ratio and low oral bioavailability are two descriptions of one property, which is why the drug lists in 7.3 and this section overlap so heavily.

Clinical anchor

Sublingual glyceryl trinitrate exists because oral GTN is almost entirely destroyed on first pass. Buprenorphine is given sublingually for the same reason. The rectal route is deliberately unreliable in this respect: absorption from the lower rectum drains into the inferior rectal veins and bypasses the liver, while the upper rectum drains portally, so the fraction escaping first pass depends on how far the suppository travelled.

AUC as a readout of clearance

AUC  =  F × Dose / Cl Total exposure over time depends only on how much arrived and how fast it was removed. It does not depend on volume of distribution.

That independence is worth noting. Vd shapes the concentration-time curve, making it higher and shorter or lower and longer, but it does not change the area beneath it. Only the amount delivered and the clearance do.

A patient on oral morphine is converted to intravenous. Why is the IV dose smaller?

Oral bioavailability is roughly 25% because morphine is high-extraction and loses most of the absorbed dose on first pass. An intravenous dose bypasses this entirely, so approximately one third of the oral dose produces equivalent systemic exposure. Failing to make this conversion is a recognised cause of opioid overdose.

Why does AUC not depend on volume of distribution?

Because AUC integrates concentration over all time, and total exposure is determined by how much drug entered and how fast it was removed. A large Vd spreads the same quantity of drug over a lower concentration for a longer duration, and the two effects cancel exactly in the integral.

MechanismAn extraction ratio of 0.8 removes 80% on first pass, capping bioavailability at 20% even with complete absorption. High extraction and low oral bioavailability are the same property.
